Vendor Consolidation & Network TCO

A typical estate is MPLS plus edge routers, branch firewalls, VPN concentrators, a web gateway, CASB/DLP bolt-ons and an SD-WAN overlay — each with its own licence cycle, hardware refresh, console and skills gap. The real cost hides in integration, patching and cross-vendor outage triage. This use case shows how the stack collapses into one platform: fewer boxes, fewer renewals, fewer consoles — and one place to run it all.

Business objective

Why this matters

Nobody signs one contract for "the network". They sign an MPLS carriage contract, a router refresh, a firewall renewal, a VPN concentrator support agreement, an SWG licence, a CASB subscription, a DLP add-on and an SD-WAN overlay — each from a different vendor, on a different anniversary, managed in a different console by whoever holds that vendor's certification. Every one of those lines looks defensible on its own. Together they are the most expensive way to run a WAN.

The TCO argument here is deliberately structural, not numeric: you don't need a spreadsheet of assumed percentages to see that fewer boxes mean fewer refreshes, fewer renewals mean fewer negotiations, and fewer consoles mean fewer specialists, fewer integration points and faster triage. The objective is simple: collapse the estate into one platform, one policy model, one console — and make the operational maths self-evident.

The problem

The estate you're actually paying for

Ask a prospect to sketch their WAN and security stack and the whiteboard fills up fast: MPLS circuits, edge routers, branch firewalls, VPN concentrators for remote access, a secure web gateway, CASB and DLP point products layered over the top, and an SD-WAN overlay bought to soften the MPLS bill. The invoices are only half the story.

The costs you can see

  • MPLS carriage and per-site circuit contracts
  • Hardware refresh cycles — routers, firewalls, concentrators
  • Licence renewals and support contracts, one per vendor
  • An SD-WAN overlay subscription bought on top of it all

The costs that hide

  • Integration engineering to make the vendors coexist
  • Patch windows multiplied across every appliance family
  • Outage triage across vendors — everyone's dashboard is green
  • A skills gap (and training budget) per console
  • Audit evidence stitched together from different log formats
Discovery question

"How many renewal dates does your network and security estate have in the next 24 months — and how many consoles does one investigation touch, end to end?" Almost nobody knows the first number without going away to count. That is the finding: the estate has grown past the point where anyone can see its whole cost.

The Cato solution

Collapse the stack into one platform

Cato converges SD-WAN and the security service edge into a single cloud service: every flow is inspected once, in a single pass, at the nearest PoP. At the branch, a Socket replaces the router-plus-firewall pair. The global private backbone replaces MPLS. The Cato Client replaces the VPN concentrator. SWG, CASB, DLP and IPS stop being products you deploy and become policy you enable. And all of it — network and security — is configured, monitored and audited in one console, the Cato Management Application (CMA).

The operational wins

The renewal calendar collapses into a single conversation. Architecture decisions stop being driven by whichever contract expires next.

📜

Every network and security change — who, what, when — is logged in CMA's Audit Trail. Audit evidence comes from one system, in one format.

🔁

New capabilities and fixes land in the PoPs, maintained by Cato. Sockets stay thin and cloud-managed — the appliance EOL treadmill simply ends.

The refresh moment is the opening

Consolidation conversations rarely start abstract — they start when a firewall estate goes end-of-life or the MPLS contract comes up. Anchor to that event, then widen the lens: replace the one renewal in front of them, and every adjacent renewal becomes optional.

Demo runbook

How to run the demo

The demo is deliberately boring in the best way: no attack simulations, just showing that everything they currently run as separate products is one coherent system in one console.

  1. Whiteboard their estate first

    Before touching CMA, draw their stack from discovery: every box, its vendor, its console, its next renewal. Leave it visible — the rest of the demo is a running comparison against that drawing.

  2. One console: network and security policy side by side

    Network → Network Rules Security → Internet Firewall

    Walk from bandwidth and path-selection rules straight into firewall policy without changing tools. Point out that both rule bases share the same sites, users and groups — one policy model, not an integration between two products.

  3. Topology: the whole estate on one map

    Monitor → Topology

    Show sites on Sockets (London DC on an X1500, Bristol Office, Madrid Office), cloud presence (an AWS vSocket site (eu-west-2) as an HA pair, the Azure vSocket hub) and remote users on the Client — all live, in one view. Contrast with the per-vendor dashboards on the whiteboard.

  4. Add a security capability without deploying anything

    Security → IPS

    Show IPS (or CASB / DLP) enabled by policy on traffic already flowing through the PoP. The line that lands: "in your current estate, this capability is a purchase order, a box, a console and a renewal — here it's a toggle."

  5. One audit trail across everything

    Administration → Audit Trail

    Filter recent changes — a network rule edited by Marcus, a firewall rule added by Priya — all attributed in one place. This is the single audit story their current estate can't tell without stitching logs from every vendor.

  6. Close on lifecycle: the treadmill ends

    Explain the upgrade model: security capabilities update PoP-side, maintained by Cato; Socket firmware is pushed from the cloud on the customer's schedule. No sizing exercise, no forklift refresh, no end-of-life notice — then hand over the firewall refresh page as the follow-up.

PoV runbook

How to prove it in the customer's tenant

Be honest about what this PoV is: consolidation has no config of its own to demonstrate. You prove TCO by aggregating evidence from the other pilots — the firewall parity tests, the TLS inspection ratios, the DLP incidents, the failover pulls — into a single consolidation evidence pack the economic buyer can read in one sitting. The success criteria below are therefore about the pack, not about toggles: an inventory built from the customer's own numbers, a coverage matrix where every row points at live pilot evidence, and a renewal overlay that turns "one platform" into "these contracts, on these dates". Agree them up front, per Running a Cato PoV — this page is that framework's wrap-up made into a deliverable.

Prerequisites — agree before week one
  • At least two technical pilots running or complete in the same account, with their own success criteria met — this pack aggregates their evidence; it cannot substitute for them. The usual sources are the PoV tabs on Consistent Security Everywhere, TLS Inspection, CASB & DLP and SD-WAN & Last-Mile Resilience.
  • A named owner for contract data — someone from procurement or vendor management who can bring renewal dates and run costs to the workshop. Without them the inventory is guesswork and the whole pack inherits the guess.
  • The no-invented-numbers rule, in writing: no savings figure enters the pack unless it comes from the customer's own inventory. Structural claims (fewer boxes, fewer renewals, fewer consoles) stand on pilot evidence; financial claims stand only on their numbers.

How to assemble the pack

  1. Run the current-state inventory workshop

    One session, before any consolidation claim is drafted. The customer builds the estate list from their own records — vendor, product, function, renewal date, annual run cost, owner. Your job is the template and the discipline, not the numbers. Anything they cannot source stays blank and is marked blank in the readout; a blank cell is more credible than an estimate with your logo on it.

  2. Build the matrix and harvest the pilot registers

    Copy the matrix above, strike the rows that do not apply, and against each remaining row pull the two best artefacts from the linked pilots' evidence registers — they were dated and filed weekly if the pilots followed the framework, so this step is collation, not archaeology.

  3. Generate the tenant reports

    Home → Reports Monitor → App Analytics

    From the report catalogue, generate the rule hit count report — it covers the Internet Firewall, WAN Firewall and Network Rules policies in one PDF, which is itself a consolidation exhibit: network and security rule activity from a single report engine. Add an App Analytics export for the pilot scope as the "what actually runs here" baseline the inventory is checked against.

  4. Extract the audit-trail record

    Administration → Audit Trail

    Filter the Audit Trail to the PoV window: every change the pilots made — network rules, firewall rules, TLSi policy, DLP rules — sits in one chronological, attributed log (the CMA keeps 12 months, viewable three months at a time). Screenshot it for the pack: this is the "one console, one audit story" claim proven on their own change history, against however many per-vendor logs the same period spans today.

  5. Overlay the renewal timeline

    Take the inventory's renewal dates for the next 24 months and mark each contract with the matrix verdict: absorb at renewal (capability proven, artefacts attached), needs a further pilot (capability plausible, not yet evidenced here), or retain (conceded gap or out of scope). The overlay is the exec's map from evidence to action — which conversations to stop renewing by default, and when.

  6. Write and deliver the readout

    One page per claim: the incumbent line item, the capability shown, the two artefacts, the renewal date, and the decision being asked for. Present it at the decision meeting booked at the workshop, with the parked-asks register as the expansion roadmap and Migration Methodology as the "what happens next" hand-off.

What good output looks like

The finished pack reads in one sitting: inventory (their numbers), matrix (every row with two dated tenant artefacts or an honest concession), renewal overlay, readout. If any row's evidence column is thin, the fix is never prose — it is another artefact from the pilot, or the row moves to "needs a further pilot".

Troubleshooting — the honest objections

⚖️

"Boost gives us 60–90% data reduction — match that?"

Concede it: Boost's byte-level deduplication and CIFS acceleration have no like-for-like Cato equivalent — Cato optimises via backbone TCP acceleration, packet-loss mitigation and routing, which is architecturally different. Mark Boost-dependent flows "retain / re-baseline", measure them during the pilot soak, and point at the mitigations on the EdgeConnect playbook. A conceded row keeps the other rows believable.

🧾

Licence boundaries blur the matrix

A row only counts if the trial licence actually covered the pilot that evidenced it — capabilities are licensed modules, not free toggles (NG Anti-Malware, for instance, requires the base Anti-Malware service). Check entitlements against every matrix row before the readout, and state in the readout which capabilities the commercial proposal must include.

📆

Contracts outlive the evidence

Proving a capability does not end the incumbent's contract — notice periods, early-exit penalties and co-termination clauses set the real exit dates. That is exactly what the renewal overlay is for: earliest sensible exit per contract, with dual-running windows planned per Migration Methodology, not a promise of day-one savings.

🕳️

"You don't replace X"

Correct — and the matrix should say so. Endpoint EDR estates, mutual-TLS portals excluded from the TLSi scope, niche appliance features: rows the platform does not absorb are marked "retain", in writing. A matrix with honest gaps survives the customer's own due diligence; a full-house matrix does not.

📸

Evidence from the wrong tenant

A demo-tenant screenshot in the pack taints every claim next to it. Every artefact must come from the customer's account, dated inside the PoV window — if a row has only demo evidence, it has no evidence, and it moves to "needs a further pilot".

👤

The inventory stalls

Renewal dates and run costs live with procurement, and if their owner was not at the workshop the spreadsheet comes back half-empty. Escalate through the sponsor for the named owner rather than filling gaps with list prices — the readout's credibility is worth the delay.

Gotchas & exit

Honest caveats
  • Never claim savings the inventory doesn't support. No assumed percentages, no industry benchmarks dressed as their numbers. If the customer has not supplied a run cost, the readout says "cost data not provided" — the structural argument (fewer boxes, renewals, consoles) still stands on the pilot evidence alone.
  • The pack is aggregation, not configuration. Its claims are only as strong as the pilots underneath — if a pilot criterion failed, the matching matrix row reports the failure, not a softer wording of it.
  • Date everything. Inventory, matrix, artefacts and overlay all carry dates; renewal data goes stale fast, and an undated pack invites the "when was this true?" question in the worst possible meeting.

Exit: this PoV adds nothing to the tenant, so there is nothing to unwind here — each technical pilot documents its own clean exit on its PoV tab. The pack itself is handed to the customer as their asset: inventory, matrix, overlay and readout become the working papers for the commercial conversation, and the parked-asks register becomes the roadmap for whatever the platform absorbs next.
