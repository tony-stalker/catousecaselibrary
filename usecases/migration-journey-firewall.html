Firewall Refresh to FWaaS: Animated Phased Journey

A firewall refresh usually buys the same posture on newer tin — and puts the next end-of-life date straight back on the calendar. This page is the animated companion to the full firewall-refresh use case: press play and watch a hardware renewal become an exit, from the first Socket at the Frankfurt interconnect hub running FWaaS in monitor mode, through the datacentre and AWS joining one CMA rulebase and wave-by-wave branch cutover, to the day the renewal quote is cancelled.

On this page
Business objective

Refresh once more, or never again

End-of-support notices force a decision anyway — the only question is whether the answer is another sizing exercise or the last one. A phased exit de-risks the choice: FWaaS runs in monitor mode alongside each appliance before it enforces anything, so every cutover is validated against live traffic and rollback is a policy toggle, not a site visit. The walkthrough below steps through the five phases; for the full argument, economics and demo runbook see Firewall Refresh to FWaaS.

The walkthrough

The last refresh, phase by phase

Three branches — Bristol, Glasgow and Madrid — plus the Frankfurt hub (the regional interconnect), the London DC and an AWS VPC each run their own ageing firewall, every box tied down to the vendor's support and renewal cycle in the lower band. The upper band is the Cato backbone, where FWaaS lives; the Frankfurt hub sits between the two — it takes the first Socket and its eBGP handoff becomes the co-existence bridge. Watch each edge's enforcement swing up to the cloud — amber while FWaaS only observes, green once it enforces — until the renewal is cancelled. At each phase

What changes, and what to notice

PhaseWhat changesWhat to notice
0 · Appliance estate Baseline — branches, the Frankfurt interconnect hub, the London DC and the AWS VPC each enforce on their own NGFW (a virtual appliance in the cloud), all tied to the vendor's support and renewal cycle. Six separately-managed rulebases have drifted apart; the renewal quote forces a decision either way.
1 · Hub bridges The Frankfurt hub gets the first Socket; its rules are imported and FWaaS runs in monitor mode alongside the appliance, while the hub's eBGP handoff bridges legacy and Cato routing. The bridge site is the regional interconnect, not the DC — it already sees cross-estate traffic, so co-existence routing needs only the one eBGP handoff. Nothing is enforced from the cloud yet.
2 · DC & cloud Frankfurt cuts over. The London DC's HA Socket starts its monitor run beside the firewall pair, and AWS eu-west-2 gets a vSocket — cloud egress moves onto the same rulebase. The cloud DC is the early, low-risk win — no rack visit, no downtime window — and the CMA rulebase now spans hub and cloud while it forms.
3 · Branch waves Bristol and Glasgow cut enforcement over to FWaaS; the DC pair's functions split — internet/WAN policy to FWaaS, east-west segmentation to LAN Firewall; Madrid starts its monitor run. Per-box policies converge into one CMA rulebase as each wave lands; signed-off appliances drop to powered-on standby — the dormant ties are the rollback path.
4 · Renewal cancelled Madrid's soak period ends and it cuts over; the support renewal is cancelled and the hardware is removed. One global policy for every edge, DC and cloud, enforced in the PoP — nothing left to size, patch or EOL.
Why monitor-first takes the fear out of it

Every appliance keeps enforcing until its site's FWaaS policy has been proven against real traffic — the events tell you exactly what the cloud rulebase would have allowed or blocked before it decides anything. Cutover is then a policy change, and rollback is the same change in reverse; the appliance is still racked and licensed until the wave is signed off. The choreography of the hub bridge, waves, co-existence and rollback design is the Migration Methodology page; the appliance economics and the demo runbook are on Firewall Refresh to FWaaS.
