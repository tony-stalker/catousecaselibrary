Demo Segment: ZTNA

Segment 5 of the 60-minute platform demo — minutes 30 to 35. Five minutes on identity-aware, posture-checked access from the Cato Client: the user as the IdP knows her, the device check the Client runs, the rule that allows her to one private application and the rule that denies the rest. Same engine as the four segments before it, now applied to traffic coming in to your applications rather than going out to the internet.

On this page
Why this segment

The sentence they must leave with

Access to a private application is decided by who the user is and the state of the device she is on — continuously, by the same engine that just inspected her internet traffic — and nothing she has not been explicitly allowed is reachable.

Legacy remote access gives a credential the whole network: once the tunnel is up, the VPN has done its job and lateral movement is the user's problem to resist. Cato replaces that with a single user identity provisioned from the IdP, so a policy written once is enforced whether the user is behind a site or remote, paired with a Device Posture Profile the Cato Client evaluates on the endpoint. Checks inside a profile combine with AND and, by account default, re-run continuously every 10 minutes, so a device that drifts out of compliance mid-session is disconnected rather than trusted until tomorrow's login.

This segment shows the Socket-site path: the application sits behind a Cato Socket in the London DC, and the WAN Firewall is the rulebase that allows or denies the flow. The next segment, App Connectors, covers the applications you do not want to on-ramp a whole network for. Keep the two apart in the room — same identity, same posture, same engine, two ways for the application to be reachable.

The five-minute story

Bridge in, three beats, bridge out

Bridge in — from DLP

“So far every flow was a user going out. Now the other direction: users reaching your private applications.”

  1. Beat 1 — Who, and on what

    Say: “Every private-access decision starts with two facts the platform already holds: the user, as your IdP provisioned her, and the device, as the Client sees it right now.”

    Show: Priya under Access → Users — SCIM-provisioned, with her IdP groups, the same identity whether she is at a desk behind a Socket or at home on the Client. Then the Device Posture Profile under Resources → Device Posture: the checks it bundles and that they combine with AND.

  2. Beat 2 — The rule that allows and the rule that denies

    Say: “Least privilege is two rules, not two hundred: one that names the user group, the posture profile and the one application, and the implicit block underneath it that catches everything else.”

    Show: Access → Client Connectivity Policy — the front door: a device that fails the profile never establishes a tunnel. Then Security → WAN Firewall: the allow rule scoped to Priya's group plus the profile, reaching only the ERP application behind the London DC Socket; nothing beneath it but the implicit block.

  3. Beat 3 — The proof in events

    Say: “Here is the same person, twice: allowed from the managed laptop, denied from the one that fails the check. The allow carries her name, the rule, the action and the destination — not an IP address; the block carries her name, the policy and the check she failed.”

    Show: Monitor → Events filtered to Priya: the allow against the named WAN Firewall rule beside the block from the non-compliant device, reason “Device fails to meet the Device Check”.

What the engine does

Identity and posture decide; the WAN Firewall enforces

The Cato Client connects Priya to the nearest PoP, where the single-pass engine already knows her identity and group from SCIM and her device state from the posture profile the Client has just evaluated. The Client Connectivity Policy decides whether a tunnel exists at all; the WAN Firewall then decides, rule by rule, top-down and first-match, what that tunnel may reach. The same user on a device that fails the profile fails the Client Connectivity rule, so no tunnel is established — she never reaches the WAN Firewall — and the Client names the failed check, with a Client Connectivity Policy block event behind it.

One identity, two devices, two outcomes. The failing device is stopped at the Client Connectivity Policy: no tunnel is established and it never reaches the WAN Firewall. The faint lines are the control plane: SCIM-provisioned groups and the posture profile are the objects the rules reference, and the implicit block sits beneath rule 1 for any tunnel that matches nothing.

Posture without another agent

🔁

Continuous, not login-only

Least privilege as a rulebase

WAN Firewall rules name a group, a posture profile and one destination over a final implicit ANY ANY block — the deny is the absence of an allow, and it is logged.

Demo runbook

Five minutes, four screens

  1. The user as the IdP knows her 1 min

    Access → Users

    Say: “Priya was never created in this console — SCIM provisioned her and her groups from your IdP in near real time, and if she leaves, the same sync disables her.”

    Show: Priya's user record: origin SCIM, the directory named as source, her group membership — the Finance group the rule in step 3 will reference.

  2. The device check the Client runs 1 min

    Resources → Device Posture → Profiles Resources → Device Posture → Check Policy

    Say: “This is the endpoint baseline as an object: anti-malware running, disk encrypted, patched. All of these must be true — and the Client re-checks every ten minutes, not once at login.”

    Show: The corporate profile and its checks; point at the AND between them, then the Check Policy tab for the continuous-check setting and its interval.

  3. The rule that allows, the rule that denies 2 min

    Access → Client Connectivity Policy Security → WAN Firewall

    Say: “Two gates. The first decides whether a tunnel exists at all — the profile is a condition of connecting. The second decides what the tunnel may reach: one rule, Finance plus the profile, to the ERP application behind the London DC Socket. Everything else falls to the implicit block.”

    Show: The Client Connectivity rule with the posture profile attached and action Allow WAN and Internet, then the WAN Firewall allow rule — source group, posture profile as criteria, destination the ERP host, tracking set to Events — and the empty space beneath it.

  4. Same person, allowed and denied 1 min

    Monitor → Events

    Say: “Here is Priya twice. From the managed laptop: the allow, named rule, destination. From the personal laptop: a block, Sub-Type Client Connectivity Policy, reason ‘Device fails to meet the Device Check’. That pair is your audit trail for least privilege.”

    Show: Events filtered to Priya's identity — the allow and the block side by side — the allow carrying user name, rule, action and destination; the block carrying user name, Sub-Type Client Connectivity Policy and the unmet check (no destination, because no flow was attempted).

If they ask

Likely questions and the two-sentence answer

“Is this just a VPN with a different name?”

A VPN gives a tunnel the network; here the Client reaches the nearest PoP and the WAN Firewall decides per rule what the identity may reach, over an implicit block. When the same user sits in the office the Client switches to riding the Socket and hits exactly the same rulebase — there is no second policy for remote users to drift from.

Securing the Hybrid Workforce — one front door

“What happens if the device goes non-compliant mid-session?”

The Check Policy re-runs the profile continuously — every 10 minutes by default, configurable between 5 and 1,440 — and a device that fails while connected is disconnected with a Details button naming the unmet check. Each failure creates a block event with Sub-Type Client Connectivity Policy, so the record exists beside the rule. Continuous checking is supported on Windows and macOS Clients; confirm the tenant's Check Policy tab before the demo, as a tenant may have changed the default.

ISO 27001 — posture as the enforcement gate

“Do we need to deploy another agent for posture?”

No — the Cato Client already on the device evaluates the Device Checks, and third-party anti-malware, firewall, patch management and endpoint DLP agents are recognised through the OPSWAT library, so your current EDR is a selection rather than an integration. An Intune MDM compliance state can also be a check.

ISO 27001 — the eleven check types

“How do the groups get here, and what about leavers?”

SCIM from the IdP creates, updates and disables Cato users and syncs the groups policy references, in near real time; unassigning a user in the IdP disables them in Cato. Build purpose-named flat groups for Cato to consume and change-control them like firewall rules — nested groups are not supported over SCIM.
