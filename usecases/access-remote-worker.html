Remote Worker Experience: Per-User, Per-App Assurance

Estate dashboards average away the one person who is actually having a bad day. This use case follows a single remote worker — from onboarding, through the always-on Cato Client and nearest-PoP egress, to the morning they say “it is slow” — and shows how per-user, per-app Experience Monitoring turns that sentence into an evidence-backed fix: last mile, backbone or the app itself, named in one screen.

Business objective

Why this matters

Two other pages in this library carry the wide-angle versions of this story: Securing the Hybrid Workforce owns the access policy — one consistent, secure front door for every user — and Proactive Digital Experience Monitoring owns the organisation-wide view: sites, probes, path analysis and anomaly detection across the estate. This page deliberately zooms all the way in: one named worker, one laptop, one home connection — because that is the granularity at which remote work is actually experienced, and at which support tickets are actually raised.

The objective: for any individual remote worker, at any moment, be able to say what their experience of each application is, and — when it degrades — which segment of their path is responsible. Not “the network looks fine”; a per-user, per-app answer with numbers behind it.

The problem

One user, zero evidence

Estate-level monitoring is built to spot patterns — a degraded site, a failing circuit, a SaaS outage. A single remote worker generates no pattern. Their home Wi-Fi, their ISP and their laptop are invisible to corporate tooling, so when they report a problem the investigation starts from nothing.

The anecdote trap

A remote worker's ticket is one person's word against a green dashboard. With no telemetry from the home, the helpdesk falls back on ritual: reboot, speed test, “is anyone else seeing this?” — and because nobody else is, the ticket closes as no fault found while the worker's day stays slow.

The per-app blind spot

“Slow” is rarely uniform. One SaaS app can crawl while everything else is fine — an app-side problem — or every app can degrade together, which points at the device or the last mile. Without per-app scores for that one user, the two cases are indistinguishable, and each gets escalated to the wrong team.

Discovery question

“Pick one home worker who called the helpdesk last week. Could you see what their Microsoft 365 experience was that morning — as a number, per application — or only what the service health page said for everyone?”

The worker's journey

Brandon, from day one to steady state

Meet Brandon — a fictional remote worker on the US west coast, hired by a UK-headquartered organisation that never intends to put a VPN concentrator between him and his work. His journey is four decisions, each made once in policy and enforced automatically ever after:

The three policies behind the quiet steady state

Always-On

An ordered rule base ties users and groups to mandatory Cato connectivity, so traffic is always inspected. Escape hatches are deliberate, not accidental: admin-issued one-time bypass codes (valid up to 15 minutes), user-initiated bypass with a recorded reason (Windows and macOS Clients, iOS v5.6+; on Windows v5.9+ the bypass length follows the rule's Disconnect Duration), and a recovery mode that decides whether internet access continues if the Cato Cloud is unreachable. Not supported on Linux Clients.

Split tunnel & egress

Default: all traffic through the tunnel to the nearest PoP for full inspection. The Split Tunnel Policy is an ordered rule base with two deliberate alternatives — exclude mode (everything to Cato except named exceptions) and include mode (only named traffic to Cato) — scoped by user or group, OS, country and managed/unmanaged network, matching IP ranges, domains, applications and ports, with local DNS resolution for chosen domains.

Connectivity + posture

The Client Connectivity Policy checks the device before it joins: posture profiles bundle device checks with AND logic — anti-malware present, disk encryption on, patches current (Windows and macOS) — alongside platform, country and authentication confidence level. A device that stops complying drops to secured-internet-only access or is blocked, per rule.

The point for the room: Brandon never files a “please set up my VPN” ticket, never picks a gateway from a dropdown, and never works uninspected. The same policies also generate the telemetry the next section runs on — the Client and the PoP see every packet of his working day.

The Cato solution

When Brandon says “it is slow”

Six months in, Brandon messages the helpdesk: “Office 365 has been slow all morning.” Because his Client, his nearest PoP and the backbone between them are one platform, every segment of his path is already instrumented — the answer is a look-up, not an investigation. The diagram shows where the measurements come from:

Encrypted tunnel — Client to nearest PoP, then backbone to each appSaaS egress at the PoP
One platform observes every hop of Brandon's path — so a Fair score can be pinned to a segment. Loss at ② with a clean ③ and ④ is his ISP; a slow ④ with ①–③ green is the application's side of the fence.

In the CMA that logic lands on one page: Brandon's user drill-down in Experience Monitoring. The summary strip identifies the session — user, site (Remote User), last connected PoP and ISP, plus his device's OS, Client version and internal/external IPs. The Select Application widget ranks his top applications by usage with an average experience score for each, and selecting one reveals its per-app measurements: Time to First Byte, TCP connect, TLS connect, HTTP latency and HTTP/S errors. For Zoom, Teams and Webex, a meetings widget breaks each call down by audio, video and screen sharing — packet loss, jitter, latency and bitrate.

When the complaint is “everything is slow” rather than one app, the Connection Details view decomposes his path into scored nodes — client device, Wi-Fi, LAN gateway, last mile (tunnel) and application — each independently rated against published thresholds: CPU Good below 70% utilisation, Wi-Fi Good at −67 dBm or stronger, LAN gateway Good below 1% loss and 10 ms, and the last-mile tunnel Good below 1% loss and 20 ms. The first non-green node names the fault domain; a tunnel-age widget adds context without skewing the score.

🧑‍💻

Per-user, per-app truth

Every sanctioned app Brandon uses carries its own Good / Fair / Poor score — so “slow” resolves to which app, how much, since when before anyone remotes into his laptop.

📍

Segment isolation in one screen

Device, Wi-Fi, LAN gateway, last-mile tunnel and application are scored as separate nodes — last mile vs backbone vs app-side is a glance, not a war room.

🔁

Telemetry as a by-product

No extra agent, no synthetic-only view: the always-on Client and the PoP that carry Brandon's traffic are the sensors, so coverage is exactly his working day.

🧾

Evidence for every audience

TTFB and flow counts for the app team, tunnel loss for the ISP call, posture and block events for security — one drill-down feeds them all.

Demo runbook

How to run the demo

Before you demo — dig into the “why”

Ask how remote-worker complaints reach IT today and what the last one cost to resolve. Then run the whole demo as one person's story — pick a remote user in the tenant and stay with them. Estate-wide DEM (sites, probes, path analysis, anomalies) is a different conversation: hand that to Proactive Digital Experience Monitoring rather than mixing the two.

  1. Find your worker

    Home → Experience Monitoring

    Open the Remote Users tab and sort by experience, worst first. Pick one user — your “Brandon” — and open their drill-down. Frame it: “this is one employee's working day, as the platform saw it.”

  2. Read the summary strip

    Walk the header: site Remote User, network access, the count of scored apps with the Good / Fair / Poor split, the last connected PoP and the ISP. Point out that nobody deployed anything extra to get this — it is the same Client that enforces policy.

  3. Filter to the app they'd complain about

    In Select Application, choose the busiest degraded app (in the capture above, Microsoft Office 365 at Fair). Show the per-app numbers — average TTFB, data up/down, flow count — and the score graph against the all-apps line. Add the Connection Type Is Remote filter to keep the view to remote sessions; remove it to blend in any office-side activity the same user had behind a Socket.

  4. Isolate the segment in Connection Details

    Open Connection Details and walk the nodes left to right — client device (CPU, memory), Wi-Fi (signal strength), LAN gateway, last-mile tunnel (loss and latency, upstream and downstream), application. Hover the first non-green node and read its averages: that is the fault domain, decided by thresholds rather than opinion.

  5. Add the connection and posture context

    Show the Devices panel — OS, Client version, internal and external IP, ISP — then the events on the score graph: user connected, user authenticated, and any security block events for the very app under discussion. Segue: the same platform that measures Brandon also decides what his device may do.

  6. Close on the policies that made it automatic

    Access → Client Connectivity Policy

    Show the Always-On rules (with their controlled bypass options), the posture profiles referenced by the connectivity rules (Access → Device Posture), and the default-full-tunnel rule base in Access → Split Tunnel Policy. Land the message: Brandon's assured experience is policy output, not heroics — it was decided once, on day one.

PoV runbook

How to prove it in the customer's tenant

The demo told Brandon's story in our tenant; the PoV re-runs it on the customer's own remote workers — an experience-assurance pilot that is observe-only by construction. Nothing in this runbook enforces anything: the posture, always-on and one-policy proofs belong to the companion Securing the Hybrid Workforce PoV, and the estate-wide view (sites, probes fleet-wide, anomalies) to Operational & Executive Visibility. This one proves a single sentence: for any pilot user who says "it is slow", the platform names the fault domain, evidences the fix and answers "is it just me" — from the console. Prerequisites — agree before week one
  • DEM licence — Cato's documentation is explicit that a separate DEM licence is required for DEM pages and features (What is Cato Experience Monitoring). Confirm the trial entitlement before any criterion is signed.
  • Current Clients on the cohort — the widgets in this runbook are version-gated: user DEM analytics need Windows Client v5.12.10+ / macOS v5.7+, the device, Wi-Fi and LAN gateway nodes in Connection Details appear only for Windows and macOS users on the Cato Client (no separate version is documented beyond that DEM floor), and underlay probe data for remote users needs Windows v5.17 / macOS v5.10. Standardise the pilot on current Clients and the whole page lights up.
  • Cohort — 10–20 genuinely remote workers, deliberately including a couple of known complainers: this PoV needs at least one real "it is slow" to land during the pilot window.
  • Identity connected — drill-downs must carry names, not IPs; IdP provisioning per Identity Integration Design.
  • Policy stays out of scope — no posture, always-on or split-tunnel changes in this runbook; if the customer wants those proven too, run the hybrid-workforce PoV alongside rather than blending the two.
  • Optional socket site — only needed if the per-hop traceroute artefact is in the criteria: path-analysis traceroute runs from the Socket (v25+), not from a home laptop.

Configuration walkthrough

  1. Switch on the Experience Monitoring policy, scoped to the cohort

    Home → Experience Monitoring Probes

    Enable the Experience Monitoring Policy toggle, then add a User rule whose source is the pilot group — user rules can additionally scope by platform and country, and the rulebase is ordered, first match wins. Each rule takes between one and 20 probes; user probes run at a default 300-second interval (Configuring the Experience Monitoring Probes and Policy).

  2. Define probes for the apps they would actually complain about

    Home → Experience Monitoring Probes

    Create probes for the cohort's real working set — the productivity suite, the UC app, one private application behind the datacentre socket. Each probe is a destination (URL or IP, classified Internet or WAN) plus a protocol: ICMP, TCP, HTTP/HTTPS or DNS. Probe traffic traverses the security policy like any other traffic and can be blocked by an existing rule — worth knowing before you diagnose a silent probe. Nothing here enforces anything: probes observe.

  3. Capture the week-one baseline, per user

    Home → Experience Monitoring

    On the Remote Users tab, capture the cohort view — experience score, app count, connected PoP, flows and average TTFB per user — then open a drill-down for each cohort member and capture the Select Application widget with its per-app scores and measurements (TTFB, TCP connect, TLS connect, HTTP latency, HTTP/S errors). Scores are relative to expected experience, so let the baseline settle for a few days before reading meaning into a Fair (Using the Experience Monitoring Page, the user drill-down page).

  4. Rehearse the complaint drill before the complaint arrives

    Home → Experience Monitoring

    Practise the walk you will run live: summary strip → Select Application → per-app measurements → Connection Details, reading the five nodes left to right against their published thresholds — CPU Good below 70% and memory below 60%, Wi-Fi Good at −67 dBm or stronger, LAN gateway Good below 1% loss and 10 ms, last-mile tunnel Good below 1% loss and 20 ms. Each node is scored independently, and the tunnel-age widget is context only — it never skews the score (Experience Monitoring Connection Details).

  5. Take the real complaint end-to-end — then time the fleet-wide check

    Home → Experience Monitoring

    When the genuine ticket lands, run the drill live with the customer watching and save every screen, dated, into the evidence pack: the per-app score that names the app, the Connection Details node that names the segment. Then answer "is it just me?" — back to the Remote Users tab, sort worst-first, filter to the application, read the cohort. Time it with a stopwatch; the elapsed minute is itself the artefact for criterion four.

  6. Fix it, re-capture, and build the escalation pack once

    Home → Experience Monitoring

    Remediate the named fault and take the "after" capture in the same Connection Details view — a Fair Wi-Fi node (say, −72 dBm on a congested band) back to Good after moving the user's AP or band is the cleanest before/after in the library. Windows Client v6.0+ even nags the user directly below −75 dBm, no DEM licence required for the notification (Experience Monitoring in the Cato Client). If the verdict was last mile instead, assemble the ISP pack: the tunnel's upstream and downstream loss and latency against thresholds, plus the ISP name and external IP from the Devices panel — and where a socket site is in scope, the per-hop Command Line table (Using Traceroute for Path Analysis).

What good output looks like

Troubleshooting the pilot

🪫

Drill-down is blank or "No score"

Check in order: the DEM licence entitlement on the trial, the Experience Monitoring Policy toggle, then whether any User rule actually matches the cohort member — the rulebase is first-match, and a user matched by no rule is simply not measured.

🧩

Device, Wi-Fi or LAN nodes missing

Connection Details shows the last-mile and application nodes but nothing device-side. Usually that is the Client, not a fault: those nodes appear only for users on the Cato Client for Windows or macOS, above the DEM floor of Windows v5.12.10 / macOS v5.7, and remote-user underlay probe data needs Windows v5.17 / macOS v5.10. Upgrade the cohort rather than annotating around the gap.

🤔

Score says Good, user says slow

Scores are relative to expected experience and averaged over the window — a Good day can hide a bad hour. Narrow the time range around the complaint, read the per-app measurements rather than the chip, and check the score graph's event annotations: a run of security block events can be the real story, as in this page's centrepiece capture.

📵

A probe never answers

Probe traffic traverses the security policy like user traffic — an existing firewall rule can block it, and the block lands in Events. Check there before blaming the target, and remember user probes fire every 300 seconds by default: curves build in minutes, not seconds.

📉

Per-app metrics missing for one app

Application metrics are calculated for outbound TCP traffic, and the Select Application widget ranks the user's top applications by usage — a rarely used app may not chart. Data can also lag: allow up to 30 minutes (Teams meeting data likewise) before concluding the widget is broken.

📶

Half the cohort's Wi-Fi shows Fair

The −67 dBm boundary catches a lot of real home setups — that is the threshold working, not a false positive. Do not chase the whole cohort: pick the one complaint user for the before/after criterion, fix their band or AP placement, and present the rest as the baseline's honest picture of home working.

Gotchas & exit

Say these out loud before week one
  • The DEM licence is a boundary, not a footnote — agree what the trial includes before criteria are signed, not when a page renders empty.
  • Scores are relative — Good/Fair/Poor measures deviation from the expected experience, not an SLA. Put the absolute numbers — TTFB in ms, loss in %, dBm — in the evidence pack, never just the colours.
  • Traceroute is socket-side — the per-hop Traceroute and Command Line views run from Sockets (v25+); a purely remote laptop evidences its last mile through the Connection Details tunnel readings. Do not promise an MTR table from a home sofa.
  • A real complaint cannot be scheduled — the criterion says "during the pilot", and the cohort includes known complainers for a reason. Agree a synthetic fallback in the register up front (a deliberately throttled sacrificial laptop), used only if the window closes quietly.
  • Version gates move with releases — the Client thresholds quoted here were last re-checked against the KB on 31 Oct 2026; re-check before quoting them in a proposal.

Unwinding cleanly: disable the Experience Monitoring Policy toggle, or delete the pilot User rule and its probes — nothing else was changed, because nothing was enforced. Export the evidence pack first: the weekly baselines, the complaint-to-verdict drill-down set, the before/after pair and the ISP pack outlive tenant access. If the decision is a yes, keep the rule and widen the source from the pilot group to the remote workforce — the pilot's probe set is the production starting point.
