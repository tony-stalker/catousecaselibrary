SonicWall Policy Migration

Swapping a TZ for a Socket is a routing exercise — the SonicOS policy estate is where the project earns its keep. This page works domain by domain through the SonicWall policy stack: zone-matrix access rules and the NAT policies beside them, CFS web filtering, App Control and DPI-SSL, and the security services behind them — showing what each construct becomes in the Cato Management Application, the semantic traps in between, and the four-phase pipeline that rationalises the rulebase on hit counters before anything is translated. It also carries a rule the other policy pages don't need: after the 2024–25 SSL-VPN campaigns and the MySonicWall cloud-backup breach, every credential in the export is treated as compromised — rotated at migration, never ported.

Business objective

Why policy translation is the long pole

Sockets ship, subnets move, sites cut over — and then the project meets years of accumulated SonicOS policy: a zone matrix per box across a TZ-heavy branch estate, near-identical CFS policy variants per user population, a NAT table full of forgotten port-forwards, and an SSLVPN zone whose rules terminate on the most attacked surface in the estate. Translating that badly does more damage than translating it slowly: lift-and-shift faithfully reproduces every dead rule, every any-any exception — and, on this estate, every carried-over credential — on a brand-new platform. The operational muscle memory the team brings (zone thinking, per-box rules) survives as intent: same intent, one global policy, no boxes to patch.

The Cato Professional Services approach is a four-phase pipeline, applied per policy domain: EXPORT the configuration and its usage evidence → REVIEW & MAP — rationalise on per-rule traffic counters, discard dead weight, then map surviving intent to Cato constructs → DEPLOY monitor-first, so every translated rule earns evidence before it can hurt anyone → OPTIMISE against events and hit counts until the policy is demonstrably right. Review-and-map, not lift-and-shift: per-box rule bases collapse into one global policy, and the clean-up is what makes that mapping tractable.

This page covers the policy workstream only. The network side — parallel Sockets alongside the TZs, the IPsec on-ramp for retained SonicWalls, remote-access cohorts off NetExtender, and the EOS-driven wave plan — is the companion playbook, SonicWall to Cato.

Rule zero

Treat the export as compromised — rotate, never port

On most estates the config export is just a mapping input. On a SonicWall estate it is also potential breach material, and SonicWall's own advisories say so. CVE-2024-40766 (improper access control in SonicOS management access and SSL-VPN, CVSS 9.3, advisory 22 August 2024, added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 9 September 2024) came with an instruction that matters here: reset all local SSL-VPN account passwords — especially for configurations imported from Gen 6 to Gen 7. A year later, SonicWall's notice on the Gen 7 SSL-VPN campaign (published 4 August 2025, updated through 22 August) concluded with “high confidence” that the activity was not a zero-day but correlated with that same CVE — fewer than 40 incidents under investigation, many tied to Gen 6 → Gen 7 migrations where local user passwords were carried over and never reset. NHS England issued cyber alert CC-4686 on the same activity. Then the MySonicWall cloud-backup breach: SonicWall's 17 September 2025 notice disclosed unauthorised access to firewall configuration backup files, and on 8 October 2025 SonicWall — investigating with Mandiant — concluded that every customer who had used the cloud backup service was affected. The files contain encrypted credentials plus full configuration data, and SonicWall's notice says possession “could increase the risk of targeted attacks”. The prescribed remediation was an “Essential Credential Reset” across local users, TOTP bindings and IPsec VPN keys.

The third-party reporting — attribute it as reporting, not as SonicWall statements — completes the picture: Arctic Wolf reported roughly 30 Akira and Fog ransomware intrusions from August 2024 with initial access via SonicWall SSL-VPN accounts, and a second wave from 22 July 2025 against patched Gen 7 firewalls, largely on credentials carried over from Gen 6 imports. The Hacker News and Rapid7 reported malicious logins that passed OTP MFA challenges — consistent with seeds and credentials harvested earlier — with encryption in under four hours. November 2025 press reporting said SonicWall had confirmed a state-sponsored actor behind the backup incident; SonicWall's own notice says only “unauthorized party”. The structural lesson for a policy page: the exploitation mechanic was configuration carry-over — old secrets imported into new boxes. This migration must not repeat it on a third platform.

What rule zero means for every domain below:

  • Enumerate the secrets in the export during discovery: local user accounts (especially SSL-VPN-enabled), IPsec pre-shared keys, SNMP strings, admin accounts, TOTP bindings.
  • Rotate everything at migration. No credential, key or community string from the SonicWall estate is re-used on the Cato side — or left live on retained SonicWalls once its Cato replacement exists.
  • Never recreate local VPN accounts. All Cato access is IdP-backed with MFA and device posture; the SSLVPN-zone rulebase is re-authored against identity, not translated.
  • Handle the exports as sensitive artefacts. The TSR is the running config in plain text — restrict where the mapping workbook lives, and destroy working copies when the project closes.
  • Log the rotation as evidence. Local accounts removed and PSKs retired is an auditable outcome — measurable against SonicWall's own credential-reset guidance — and delete the MySonicWall cloud backups once the appliances are retired.
Keep it fair

SonicWall published its advisories promptly, shipped SonicOS 7.3 hardening, and brought in Mandiant. The argument is not vendor character assassination — it is that VPN credentials, PSKs and full configs lived on and around WAN-facing appliances and the vendor's cloud, and both were compromised classes in 2024–25. The migration's job is to make that entire secret class disappear, not to move it.

Domain 1 — FW rules

Zone-matrix access rules & the NAT policies

The construct. SonicOS classic mode — the norm on TZ and NSa — is a per-appliance zone matrix: ordered access rules per zone pair (LAN, WAN, DMZ, WLAN, VPN, SSLVPN), built from address objects and groups, service objects and user/group conditions, with App Rules layered beside them. NAT policies are their own table — egress masquerading plus the inbound port-forwards that branch estates accumulate (local servers, CCTV, building management). Gen 7 adds a wrinkle: NSsp and NSv can run “policy mode” (unified policy) while TZ/NSa estates typically stay classic — the mapping workbook has to parse both formats, but both collapse into the same CMA target.

Export. Three complementary forms per firewall — and on NSM-managed estates, one pass over the whole estate: export the device inventory to CSV/JSON, pull per-firewall configs to templates, and collect TSRs centrally.

Semantic gotchas. Zone semantics have no equivalent — and a matrix per box multiplied across a branch estate hides enormous duplication, so dedupe objects before translating rules or the sprawl is recreated on day one. The per-rule Rx/Tx counters are the rationalisation evidence, but only if they are trustworthy: clear them at the start of discovery and re-read after 30 days before declaring anything dead. The SSLVPN zone is the exception to every mapping rule on this page — its rules describe the exploited surface, so they are re-authored as identity-scoped ZTNA policy rather than mapped, and nothing behind them (accounts, portal, bookmarks) survives. And the NAT table is its own mini-workstream: most of it dissolves into PoP egress NAT, and what survives — the published services — moves on scheduled change windows.

Strategy. Pull the API export per appliance (TSRs where the API is not enabled); retire zero-traffic rules and collapse duplicate per-box objects against the counters; push the cleaned object model through the GraphQL API or Terraform first, then build rules on top of it; deploy new WAN and Internet Firewall rules monitor-first and let events prove them before enforcement.

Domain 2 — SWG policy

Content Filtering, App Control & DPI-SSL

The construct. SonicWall web filtering is CFS 4.0: profiles and policies matching users and groups against category actions, with allowed and forbidden URI lists and keyword blocking, usually accumulated as near-identical variants per user population. App Control is a separate layer: signature-based application categories with per-app actions, including bandwidth-management actions. DPI-SSL provides client and server TLS interception with a resigning CA and exclusion lists — sized against hard per-model connection limits on TZ hardware, which is why many estates never fully enabled it.

Export. CFS profiles and policies, URI lists, App Rules and the DPI-SSL exclusion lists all travel in the same config export as the access rules; AppFlow and Analytics reports are the evidence base for what each category action actually affects.

SonicOS constructCato constructNotes
CFS category actions Cato URL categories in Internet Firewall rules Taxonomies differ in names and granularity — map by intent, review every blocked category individually
Allowed / forbidden URI lists Custom categories Consolidate per-box lists into custom categories referenced by rules
CFS keyword blocking No direct equivalent Validate whether the need still exists before promising parity — it is usually a policy relic
Per-user/group CFS policies Identity-scoped Internet Firewall rules via SCIM groups Collapse the policy variants into a small number of group-scoped rule tiers
App Control categories & signatures App and app-category rules in the Internet/WAN Firewall Cato's application catalogue is maintained in-cloud — application awareness is native to the rule
App Rules bandwidth actions Cato QoS / bandwidth priorities Re-express throttling intent as bandwidth priorities, not as blocks
DPI-SSL client/server inspection + resigning CA Cato TLS Inspection at the PoP The same CA-trust deployment job — swap the resigning certificate for the Cato certificate in MDM; no connection-count sizing
DPI-SSL exclusion lists TLS Inspection bypass rules Re-review rather than copy — exclusions shaped by appliance limits, not intent, should not outlive the appliance

Semantic gotchas. The taxonomy mismatch is the headline risk: a SonicWall category and a similarly-named Cato category are not guaranteed to cover the same sites, so a mechanical name-for-name mapping silently changes what is blocked — build the translation sheet by intent and monitor it before enforcing. The bigger SonicWall-specific reality cuts the other way: because DPI-SSL was gated by per-model connection limits and CA-deployment pain, the Cato TLS inspection rollout may be the customer's first full TLS inspection. Plan exclusions (banking, health) and user communications accordingly rather than assuming like-for-like — and expect the single-pass engine to inspect traffic the TZ never saw, which means new detections to triage in the monitor phase, not incidents.

Strategy. Build a category translation sheet mapping each CFS category-action to a Cato category and action by intent; consolidate the URI lists into custom categories; re-express App Control blocks as app-aware rules and bandwidth actions as QoS priorities; deploy the blocked set in monitor mode and compare events against the AppFlow baseline; then stage TLS inspection by category and pilot group once the Cato certificate is deployed through MDM.

Domain 3 — Threat prevention

Gateway AV, IPS & Capture ATP

The construct. The SonicOS security services — Gateway Anti-Virus, Anti-Spyware and IPS, with the Botnet and Geo-IP filters beside them, and Capture ATP cloud sandboxing (with RTDMI) on the boxes licensed for it. What is actually enforced per box is a licensing question before it is a config question: Gen 6 bundles were CGSS (no sandboxing) versus AGSS (adds Capture ATP); Gen 7 renamed them EPSS and APSS. Read the renewal SKUs in MySonicWall per firewall — the bundle names are the reliable discovery signal for what each site really runs today.

SonicOS constructCato constructNotes
Gateway Anti-Virus + Anti-Spyware Cato Anti-Malware in the single-pass engine An account-level layer applied to all traffic — including sites whose box was never licensed for it
IPS service Cato IPS Account-level, cloud-maintained signatures; run in monitor first and triage new detections
Botnet Filter Cato reputation-based protections The control SonicWall's Aug 2025 guidance told customers to enable — mapped to the platform layer
Geo-IP Filter Cato geo-based protections and rules Re-express country blocks as policy; review the list against current business reality
Capture ATP + RTDMI (AGSS/APSS boxes) Cato Anti-Malware / NG Anti-Malware (ML-based) Match file-type scoping and block-until-verdict behaviour expectations in the pilot — agree them, don't assume them
Comprehensive Anti-Spam (EPSS bundles) No Cato equivalent Almost always redundant with M365/Google email security — verify MX records and any on-box smart-host arrangements before promising parity

Semantic gotchas. Per-box licensing variance is the trap: a branch TZ on CGSS/EPSS has no sandboxing today, so the account-level Cato policy levels every site up to the same protections — a selling point, but also a source of new detections that must be triaged in monitor mode before anything blocks. Where Capture ATP ran with block-until-verdict semantics, walk through how the customer expects unknown files to be handled and validate the equivalent behaviour in the pilot rather than promising a like-for-like. And the anti-spam line item is the one genuine subtraction in this domain — surface it in discovery with the MX evidence, not at contract time.

Domain 4 — CASB & DLP · greenfield

Not a translation — the upgrade

There is nothing to export. The SonicOS config carries no CASB or network-DLP rulebase: SaaS governance in a SonicWall estate — where it exists at all — is App Control signatures and CFS categories doing a job they were never designed for. Frame this honestly in the room: this is not a migration gap, it is the upgrade the customer is buying. Both policies are built greenfield from Cato best practice. What the estate does contribute: AppFlow/Analytics application reporting seeds the sanctioned-app conversation, the CFS worksheet from Domain 2 flags informal SaaS blocks worth preserving as proper CASB rules — and for DLP the input is not a config file but a short data-protection workshop with the compliance owner on what data matters, where it lives, and who legitimately moves it.

In the SonicWall estateCato constructWhat changes
AppFlow / Analytics app reporting App Analytics + Cloud Apps Dashboard Continuous shadow-IT discovery with risk scores, covering sites and remote users alike
CFS/App Control doubling as SaaS control CASB application rules with granular activity controls Allow the app but govern the activity — upload, download, share — instead of a binary block
Nothing — no tenant awareness Tenant restrictions on sanctioned apps Corporate tenant allowed, personal instances of the same app controlled
Nothing — no content awareness on the wire DLP profiles with predefined data types (PCI, PII, credentials, source code) Start from the predefined types — do not hand-build patterns on day one
Nothing — no evidence trail Data Protection Dashboard + DLP events Every match attributed to user, app and data type — the audit trail the estate never had

Build sequence (best practice). Stage TLS inspection first — CASB activity control and DLP content inspection both depend on it, and on this estate that usually means enabling full inspection for the first time (Domain 2). Run shadow-IT discovery over a representative business cycle; classify the estate into sanctioned / tolerated / unsanctioned with the risk scores as evidence; write CASB and DLP rules in monitor mode; review the matches with the business and data owners; then enforce — strictest-risk apps and highest-confidence data types first. Day-one blocking is how these projects die; monitor-mode evidence is how they succeed.

Position it as the upgrade it is

“Day one on Cato you will see every SaaS app in use, scored for risk — visibility the firewall estate has never had. We turn that into control at your pace: monitor first, then enforce.” That is a selling point, not a migration risk.

Several Before rows have no After row — deliberately. The GAV and IPS services behind rule 1 are no longer per-box properties: they become the account-level Anti-Malware and IPS layers of the single-pass engine, applied to everything — including the guest network the TZ left uninspected. There is no “Corp-Web-Out” allow rule either — the Internet Firewall's default-allow posture does that job, so what survives of rule 1 is its CFS intent, as rule 3. Rule 3 of the old table exits the pipeline at REVIEW & MAP on its counter evidence. Rule 4 moves workstream (NAT, on its own change window). And rule 5 — the SSLVPN zone — leaves the rulebase entirely: its users land in Client Connectivity Policy and identity-scoped WAN Firewall rules, authenticated by the IdP with MFA, and the local accounts behind the old rule are rotated out of existence, per rule zero.

What changed in translation

  • The zone matrix became scopes and postures. LAN → WAN has no equivalent construct — the intent (which segment reaches what, under what constraints) is recovered as source scopes in one global rulebase, with the default-allow Internet posture replacing the blanket egress-allow rule.
  • Zone services became account-level layers. The CFS + GAV + IPS stack attached per zone decomposed into Internet Firewall category rules plus account-level Anti-Malware and IPS — global policy layers of the single-pass engine, with old per-box licensing gaps levelled up rather than reproduced.
  • The SSLVPN zone left the estate. Not translated, replaced: IdP-backed ZTNA with MFA and device posture — and every local account, key and seed behind the old zone rotated and deleted, never recreated.
  • The NAT table dissolved. Masquerade policies are a platform property at the PoP; only the published services survive, re-homed to remote port forwarding on their own change windows.
  • Usage evidence decided what never made the journey. The zero-traffic vendor rule was discarded at review rather than translated — the discard pile, and the rotation log, are outputs of the migration as much as the rules that survive.
Cross-domain best practices

Rules that hold across all four domains

🧭

Respect the default postures

Cato's WAN Firewall is an allowlist (implicit deny); the Internet Firewall is a blocklist (implicit allow). The zone matrix spread that decision across every zone pair per box — so egress posture is a decision, not a translation. If the customer wants allowlist egress, add an explicit final block rule; don't discover the difference in production.

🔑

Rule zero: rotate, never port

Every secret in the export — local accounts, PSKs, SNMP strings, TOTP bindings — is treated as compromised, rotated at migration, and never recreated on the Cato side. Identity comes from the IdP. The rotation log is deliverable evidence, auditable against SonicWall's own credential-reset guidance.

🔐

Stage TLS inspection deliberately

DPI-SSL was rationed by per-model connection limits, so Cato TLSi is often the customer's first full inspection. Certificates via MDM first, bypass rules for sensitive categories, pilot groups before populations — and expect new detections from traffic the TZ never saw.

🟠

Monitor, then block

Every translated rule, IPS/Anti-Malware protection, CASB control and DLP rule starts in monitor. Enforcement is earned with event evidence, per rule — the pipeline's amber-to-green transition, never a big-bang flip.

🧱

Objects before rules

Translate and dedupe the address/service object model first — the SonicOS API export is structured JSON, scriptable straight into the GraphQL API or Terraform provider — then build rules on the clean object base. Per-box object copies recreate the sprawl you just removed.

📊

Validate with events & hit counts

Clear the SonicOS per-rule counters at discovery, re-read after 30 days, and close the loop in Monitor → Events: compare each translated rule's hits against its ancestor's Rx/Tx evidence, investigate the deltas, and keep deleting never-hit rules on the new platform as ruthlessly as on the old.

The policy pipeline

EXPORT → REVIEW & MAP → DEPLOY → OPTIMISE

The same pipeline runs for each domain, offset in time — firewall first, SWG behind it, CASB and DLP once TLS inspection and discovery data are in place. On NSM-managed estates the export stage is one pass over the whole estate; unmanaged estates export box by box. Two things leave the pipeline early and never reach Cato: dead rules, discarded at review on counter evidence — and every secret in the export, rotated and replaced by IdP identity rather than mapped. The greenfield pair enters with discovery inputs instead of rulebases, and skips translation, not the monitor-first discipline.

Demo runbook

Running the policy workshop

Format

This runs best as a working session, not a show-and-tell: ask the customer to bring a TSR or SonicOS API export and 30 days of access-rule counters, and translate a few real rules live. Ask early whether MySonicWall cloud backup was ever used — the answer scopes rule zero — and treat their export as the sensitive artefact it is. The CASB/DLP answer to “what do you run today” is almost always “nothing”, which sets up the upgrade story before you open the console.

  1. Frame the pipeline — and rule zero

    Walk the EXPORT → REVIEW & MAP → DEPLOY → OPTIMISE diagram. Agree scope: which firewalls, classic versus policy mode, which domains are in use per the licence bundles (CGSS/AGSS vs EPSS/APSS), who owns the decision to retire a dead rule — and state plainly that no credential from the estate will be ported, per SonicWall's own credential-reset guidance.

  2. Show the two firewall postures

    Security → WAN Firewall Security → Internet Firewall

    One global rulebase, no zones, no per-box tables. Point out the opposite defaults — WAN allowlist, Internet blocklist — and translate one of their zone-matrix rules live, discussing which egress posture they actually want.

  3. Walk the object model and automation path

    Administration → API & Integrations

    Show hosts, groups and custom services, then the API keys page — the SonicOS API export is structured JSON (address objects, services, access rules, NAT policies), so object translation is scriptable end-to-end via the GraphQL API or the Terraform provider rather than re-keyed by hand.

  4. Translate a CFS policy

    Open an Internet Firewall rule and walk the category picker against one of their CFS profiles: map two or three categories by intent, show a custom category absorbing their allowed/forbidden URI lists, and an app-aware rule doing what App Control did. Be honest about keyword blocking — no direct equivalent; validate whether the need still exists.

  5. Stage TLS inspection — the fidelity prerequisite

    Security → TLS Inspection

    Walk the staged policy — bypasses first, then Inspect rules by category and pilot group — and be explicit that for an estate that rationed DPI-SSL against TZ connection limits, this is likely their first full TLS inspection: certificate via MDM, sensitive categories bypassed, communications planned.

  6. Show CASB and DLP as the destination state

    Monitor → Cloud Apps Dashboard Security → DLP Configuration

    Show shadow-IT discovery running with zero configuration, a tenant-control rule, and a DLP rule combining a predefined data type with a file-type condition — the greenfield domains their firewall never had.

  7. Close on the evidence loop

    Monitor → Events

    Filter events to a single monitor-mode rule and show the hit evidence that would justify flipping it to block — the OPTIMISE phase they will live in for the first months. Close on the two auditable outcomes: the discard pile from rationalisation, and the rotation log showing local accounts removed and PSKs retired.
