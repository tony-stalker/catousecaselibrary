Global Expansion & China Connectivity

When the business opens in a new region, the network is usually the last thing to arrive — carrier contracts, shipped firewalls and staging engineers turn a market entry into a quarters-long project. This use case shows how a new site lands on the nearest of 80+ Cato PoPs with the full security stack and WAN optimisation from day one — and how China, the hardest case of all, is handled compliantly through Cato's in-country presence with licensed local partners.

Business objective

Why this matters

Expansion is a board-level event: a new sales office in Madrid, a plant in South Africa, a joint venture in Sydney, a manufacturing partner in China. The commercial team signs the lease in weeks — and then discovers that the network needs carrier circuits ordered months in advance, firewalls purchased, staged and shipped, and an engineer on a plane to rack it all. IT becomes the reason the site opens late.

The objective: make the network a non-event in every market entry — connected, secured and visible on day one, with no infrastructure project per country.

The problem

The traditional new-region playbook is a project per country

Every new region traditionally restarts the same build: source MPLS or business internet from a carrier you have never worked with, or extend a DIY VPN overlay — then procure, stage and ship the security stack to sit behind it. Each step has its own lead time, its own local dependency, and its own way of slipping.

Sourcing the connectivity

  • MPLS and international circuits: long carrier lead times, per-country contracts
  • Local-loop delivery depends on providers you can't escalate
  • DIY VPN overlay rides the public internet — no predictability on long haul
  • Every region adds another routing and QoS design to maintain

Shipping the security stack

  • Firewalls purchased, licensed and staged before they ship
  • Export, import and customs logistics for network hardware
  • An engineer travels to rack, cable and cut over the site
  • Policy rebuilt per site — and it drifts from HQ from day one

The extreme case

China: where the DIY playbook breaks down

China concentrates every expansion problem into one country. Cross-border enterprise connectivity is regulated, so the connectivity itself has a compliance dimension the rest of the world doesn't. And the common DIY pattern — a VPN overlay from the China office back to Europe or the US over the public internet — suffers on both fronts: performance through congested international gateways is unpredictable, and an unlicensed overlay carries real regulatory risk for the business running it.

Cato's answer is deliberately simple to state: Cato provides compliant connectivity into and out of China through its in-country presence, operated with licensed local partners, bringing Chinese sites onto the Cato global private backbone. The site gets the same experience as everywhere else — same policy, same visibility, same console — with the regulatory side handled by partners licensed to provide it.

Positioning note — keep the China story precise

State it exactly as above: compliant connectivity into and out of China via Cato's PoPs and licensed local partners. Scope the specifics — cities, licensing detail, service options and commercials — with the Cato China team per opportunity. Don't improvise them in the room.

The Cato solution

The network is already there — the site just joins it

With Cato, expansion stops being a build and becomes an on-boarding. The backbone of 80+ PoPs is the infrastructure — it already exists in the target region, run by Cato, with the full security stack and WAN optimisation in every PoP. Opening a site means choosing an on-ramp: ship a zero-touch socket that anyone who can plug in power and WAN can install, or start on day one with the Cato Client or an IPsec tunnel while the hardware is in transit. The moment the site connects to its nearest PoP, it inherits the same policy and the same visibility as every other site in the estate.

Demo runbook

How to run the demo

Before you demo — anchor on their roadmap

Ask where the business opens next and what happened on the last opening: how long the circuit took, who staged the firewall, who flew out. Run every step below against their next market, not a hypothetical one.

  1. Create the new site before any hardware exists

    Network → Sites

    Add a site live in the room: name it after their next market, pick the socket model, set the country and native range. The site is now a policy object in the estate — created in minutes, while the physical kit hasn't even been ordered.

  2. Describe the zero-touch socket claim flow

    Narrate what happens next (no live hardware needed): the socket ships direct to the new office, anyone plugs in power and WAN, it calls home over whatever internet is available and is claimed against the site you just created — pulling configuration and policy automatically. Nobody technical travels.

  3. Show day-one connectivity while hardware is in transit

    Network → Sites

    Open aws-sydney-ipsec — a Sydney site connected over IPsec with no Cato hardware at all. Make the point: the landing team can work from day one on the Cato Client, and any existing edge device can join over IPsec, weeks before the socket arrives.

  4. Show the new region on the map

    Monitor → Topology

    Point at the spread: Madrid Office on the Madrid PoP, the Sydney IPsec site on the Sydney PoP, the UK estate on London — one map, one health view. A new region appears here the moment it connects, with the same drill-down as a site next door.

  5. Prove there is no per-site policy build

    Security → Internet Firewall

    Walk the rulebase and show that no rule mentions a region-specific firewall: the same global policy applied to Madrid the second it connected. Contrast with rebuilding and maintaining a firewall config per country.

  6. Show the experience from the new region

    Monitor → App Analytics

    Filter to Madrid Office or the Sydney site and show application experience — latency and quality to the SaaS apps the region actually uses. This is the day-two conversation answered on day one: not just connected, but measurably usable.

  7. Close on China

    No console needed — position it as in the section above: compliant connectivity into and out of China through Cato's in-country presence with licensed local partners, onto the same backbone, same policy, same visibility. Then bring in the Cato China team to scope specifics for their entity.

PoV runbook

How to prove it in the customer's tenant

The demo points at the map; the PoV lands a real site in a real new region. This is an expansion pilot: one socket shipped to the customer's next market — or the nearest thing to it they can lease a desk in — stood up by local, non-technical hands, and measured against the promise the map makes: nearest-PoP reach, day-one policy inheritance and local egress. Run it inside the frame from Running a Cato PoV: criteria agreed at the scope workshop before anything ships, every result a dated artefact on the register. Nothing here blocks or inspects content — the pilot proves reach and performance by observing and measuring. One scope note up front: China is not this pilot. It runs on the licensed-partner motion described above and is scoped with the Cato China team as its own engagement — never folded into a standard socket pilot.

Prerequisites — agree before week one
  • Scope: one socket site in the target region plus a handful of pilot remote users already working there (or travelling there for the pilot). The existing estate is untouched — it is the control group.
  • Licence agreed before the socket ships: a site left without a commercial licence ends up disabled and stops passing traffic (site licences) — the agility PoV verified this the hard way, so settle it with the commercial team at the workshop, not at power-on.
  • Shipping is part of the plan, not the criterion: network hardware crossing a border needs an importer of record, and customs clearance varies from days to weeks by country. The stopwatch starts at power-on, never at dispatch — and the landing team bridges on the Cato Client or IPsec while the box is in transit, which is itself worth demonstrating.
  • Named local hands: the person who will physically plug the socket in — office manager, serviced-office reception, the landlord's electrician — plus a phone camera for the power-on photograph. If that person needs a network qualification, the criterion has already failed.
  • Upstream allowances if the socket lands behind a local router or serviced-office edge: the outbound port and DNS allowances listed in the agility runbook's prerequisites apply unchanged — pre-clear them with whoever runs the building's network.
  • Not needed: TLS inspection, threat modules or identity integration — this pilot proves transport, reach and policy inheritance, so privacy sign-off never sits on the critical path. If a security PoV follows, stage TLSi via the staged rollout playbook.

Configuration walkthrough

  1. Confirm the region before anything ships

    Network → Sites → Site Configuration → General

    Read the target region out of Cato's published production PoP guide — it lists PoP locations and their ranges by region, flags locations that are Coming soon (which don't count for planning), and shows which PoP serves countries without a dedicated one via geo-localised ranges. Write the planned PoP into the register, then set the site's Preferred PoP Location: Cato's guidance is to keep the default Automatic and define the City, which steers the choice without fighting the connectivity algorithm (preferred PoP for a site). Avoid the restrict-to-preferred option during the pilot — it forces adherence even when that PoP has a problem.

  2. Create the site and assign the licence — weeks before the kit exists

    Network → Sites

    Create the site as in the demo runbook — name, country, socket model, native range — so it exists as a policy object while the hardware is still on order, and assign its licence at the same sitting. This is deliberately mundane: the point the register will make later is that everything the new region needed from the network team happened here, in minutes, from a desk in the UK.

  3. Ship it, and let local hands stand it up

    Monitor → Topology

    Run the zero-touch clock exactly as written in the agility runbook — power-on photograph, the witnessed Activate New Socket assignment, tunnel up, first flows — and don't restate or renegotiate it. What this pilot adds is the geography: the run is performed by the named local hands in the new region, timezone and all, with the admin clicking the activation from home. When the tunnel comes up, screenshot Topology showing the connected PoP and put it next to the prediction from step 1 — that pair is the first criterion closed.

  4. Prove inheritance with events, not assertions

    Security → Internet Firewall Home → Events

    The day before the socket powers on, capture the published rulebase, dated. The Internet Firewall is one account-level policy — rules you enable tracking on generate events, and the policy ends in an implicit any-any allow (Internet Firewall policy) — so the proof is observational: within the first hour of traffic, filter Events to the new site and show hits landing on rules that were published long before the site existed. Close the loop in Administration → Audit Trail: between site creation and first traffic there is not a single policy edit attributable to the new region. The rulebase didn't move; the site moved to it.

  5. Set the regional egress — and only where needed, override it

    Network → Network Rules

    By default the new site's internet traffic egresses at the PoP it connects to — local breakout is the starting state, not a project. The override exists for the exceptions: where a SaaS service needs a specific geography (data residency, a tenant pinned to a country, licensing), add a network rule with the NAT routing method, which "lets you egress traffic via a specific Cato allocated IP address and its PoP location" — traffic matching the rule is translated to that IP and egressed via the relevant PoP (configuring network rules). Keep the pilot honest: start with pure nearest-PoP egress, and add NAT rules only when a measured quirk (step 6 of the troubleshooting grid below) demands one — each override is itself evidence of control.

  6. Run the measurement days

    Home → Experience Monitoring Network → Sites → Site Monitoring → Real Time

    Three comparisons, captured on agreed days. Egress A/B: the same SaaS transaction — same file, same hour — from the new site via its local PoP and via the legacy backhaul path (their existing VPN or the interim hairpin), timed side by side, with the last-mile round-trip to the PoP from Real Time alongside. Remote users: the Remote Users view in Experience Monitoring with its PoP column showing the pilot users on the in-region PoP, and per-app TTFB trending across the pilot (Experience Monitoring). HQ reachability: a routine transfer from the datacentre to the new site across the backbone, visible in Topology and App Analytics, with the WAN firewall hit counts as the no-new-rules proof. Everything lands on the register dated, against its criterion, the day it happens.

What good output looks like

By the wrap-up the register holds the paired prediction-and-proof for the PoP (planned at the workshop, matched in Topology), the local-hands stand-up clocked to the agility criterion, the first-hour events landing on a rulebase nobody touched, the egress A/B table with the backhaul path losing on its own numbers, the Remote Users capture with the PoP column reading in-region, and the HQ transfer under unchanged WAN rules. Read together they make the expansion argument arithmetically: the next market is this register re-run with a different courier address — and the quarters-long project the customer budgeted for is the line that never appears.

Troubleshooting

📦

The slow bit is customs, not config

Be honest at the workshop: the longest lead item in this pilot is a box crossing a border — importer of record, duties, in-country courier handoff. None of it is product, all of it is real. Keep shipping outside the stopwatch, ship early, and let the Client/IPsec bridge carry the landing team so the pilot never waits on a customs shed.

🧱

Socket powers on but never calls home

In serviced offices and new markets the socket often sits behind someone else's edge. The outbound port and DNS allowances from the agility runbook apply unchanged — and a NAT device that rewrites source ports between socket and PoP can stop tunnels forming. Pre-clear the building network before the timed day.

📡

"The new office feels slow — is it Cato?"

The question every new-region pilot gets, usually in week two. Experience Monitoring separates the fault domains: the last-mile views isolate the local ISP loop from the tunnel and the backbone. Loss and round-trip that start at the first ISP hop, against a clean PoP, is evidence in the local carrier's own language — attribution, not argument.

🌍

SaaS turns up in the wrong language or currency

Egress-IP geolocation: the nearest PoP may sit across a border from the office, and some SaaS services key language, currency, content or even access off the egress country. The fix is step 5's NAT rule — an allocated IP in the geography the service needs, scoped to that application only. Log each quirk found; the list is valuable discovery output.

📍

Site connected to an unexpected PoP

Automatic selection weighs conditions, not just the map. Set the Preferred PoP Location — Automatic plus City per Cato's guidance — and note the socket drifts back to a preferred PoP within about an hour by default after connecting elsewhere. Resist hard-restricting to one PoP mid-pilot: it holds the site there even when that PoP has a bad day.

🪪

Site passes no traffic at all

Before debugging networking, check the licence: a site without an assigned commercial licence ends up disabled and stops passing traffic, and a scheduled licence passes nothing until it reaches staging. Thirty seconds in the site's licence assignment saves an afternoon of tunnel forensics.

Gotchas & exit

Honest caveats to set at the workshop
  • The PoP count is a moving target. This page says 80+; what matters at the workshop is the live list for their region — read it from the production PoP guide on the day, and treat Coming soon locations as exactly that: not part of the plan.
  • Nearest PoP may mean a neighbouring country. Coverage is per-PoP, not per-capital-city. Agree at the workshop which egress geography the business actually needs — proximity for latency and country-of-egress for SaaS are separate criteria, and conflating them fails both.
  • China stays out of scope. Compliant China connectivity runs through Cato's in-country presence with licensed local partners and is scoped with the Cato China team per entity — position it as the section above does and keep the standard pilot clean.
  • Capture weekly. Experience Monitoring holds at most a three-month window and near-real-time data can lag by up to 30 minutes — the egress A/B and TTFB trends go into the register at each weekly sync, dated, not reconstructed at the end.
Unwinding cleanly at the end

If the site converts to production — the usual outcome, since the office is real — the exit is simply tidying: remove any PoV-only NAT egress rules from the next published revision and reconsider the preferred-PoP settings for steady state. If it does not convert, decommission per the documented teardown sequence the agility runbook timed — references, licence, socket, then delete — returning the licence to the pool and the socket to the drawer. The register survives either way, and it walks into the decision meeting carrying the only line that matters: the next market entry is a courier label, not a project.
