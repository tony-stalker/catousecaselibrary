Zscaler (ZIA / ZPA) to Cato

ZIA and ZPA leave the customer running two products, two policy sets and an agent that only does security — with the WAN still on somebody else's kit. This use case walks the proven path onto the Cato SASE Cloud: a four-phase policy conversion, interim architectures that keep ZIA reachable while you migrate, and dual-agent co-existence that de-risks the endpoint cutover — grounded in a real customer migration with the lessons learned left in.

Business objective

Why organisations move off Zscaler

Zscaler customers run ZIA for internet security, ZPA for private access, and Zscaler Client Connector (ZCC) on every endpoint — while the WAN itself lives on separate SD-WAN or MPLS estate behind legacy firewalls. Every one of those boundaries is a policy set to maintain, a console to check and a gap where visibility ends.

The long-term strategic goal of this migration is a simplified, standard solution: one unified platform for security and networking, end-to-end visibility of every flow, and direct internet breakout from the servicing Cato PoP with SD-WAN preference routing — instead of hairpinning traffic into a separate security cloud.

The migration itself is the objection you have to beat. The rest of this page is the answer: convert policy methodically, run both clouds in parallel, and cut over one cohort at a time.

Policy conversion

Four phases from ZIA / ZPA policy to Cato policy

Cato Professional Services runs policy conversion as a four-phase process. It is deliberately not a lift-and-shift: years of accumulated Zscaler rules get cleaned up, refactored and gap-analysed against Cato best practices on the way across.

1 · Export ZIA + ZPA

ZIA: policies, URL filtering, firewall, web security, DLP, Cloud App policies, Advanced Threat Protection, SSL inspection, bandwidth control, and user/group settings.

ZPA: segment groups, access policies, Browser Access, Client Forwarding, inspection policies, trusted networks, server groups and posture profiles.

2 · Review & map Clean-up

ZIA: map to the Cato Internet firewall, App Control (tenant restrictions, CASB, DLP), Threat Prevention, SCIM groups and TLS Inspection.

ZPA: review groups and site hosts, then map to the WAN firewall and access control. Dead rules and duplicates are refactored out here, not copied across.

3 · Deploy Best practices

ZIA: implement Cato best practices — category and service restrictions, firewall, TLS Inspection, CASB, and DLP monitoring policies.

ZPA: WAN firewall, segmentation and access control best practices, Browser Access, trusted networks, and group/host configuration.

4 · Optimise Ongoing

Event analysis and performance tuning once real traffic is flowing: adjust policy restrictions where they bite, and enhance security through tighter segmentation. This is also where the gap analysis against Cato best practices pays off — the converted estate ends up cleaner than the one it replaced.

The Cato solution

Co-exist first, cut over in phases

The pattern that de-risks the whole programme is dual-agent co-existence: ZCC and the Cato Client run side by side on the same endpoint throughout the migration. On-site, the ZCC tunnel simply rides inside the Cato socket tunnel; when remote, the ZCC and Cato SDP tunnels co-exist on the workstation. Internet protection never has a gap, and either agent can be backed out independently at any phase.

On the network side, Cato PoPs keep ZIA reachable during transition: multiple active IKEv2 IPsec tunnels from the Cato cloud to ZIA, load-balanced by source/destination, egressing from the Cato PoP closest to Zscaler over the Tier-1 backbone. Sites and users move onto Cato for transport and WAN security first — internet policy flips to direct PoP breakout only once it has been converted and proven.

Interim architectures

Four ways to keep ZIA reachable during transition

Pick per site, not per customer — most migrations mix these. The first two bridge internet traffic to ZIA while Cato takes over transport; the second two phase Cato into sites that keep their existing firewall running in parallel.

IKEv2 multiple active tunnels PoP → ZIA

  • Cato builds parallel IPsec tunnels straight to ZIA — the deck's worked example runs 3 active + 3 backup
  • Backhaul load-balanced by source / destination
  • Egress from the closest Cato PoP to Zscaler, over the Tier-1 backbone
  • Support for multiple interconnects
  • Explicitly a temporary ZIA interim solution

Internet backhaul to existing FW Site FW → ZIA

  • Internet breakout stays at existing sites during transition
  • Reuses the firewall's proven GRE / IPsec connectivity into ZIA — nothing new to build on the Zscaler side
  • Load-balancing and site redundancy across breakout sites
  • Backhaul load-balanced by source / destination
  • Temporary ZIA interim solution

FW VLAN termination Phased replacement

  • Existing firewall keeps its VLANs and parallel ZIA connectivity
  • Cato joins as a transit network — BGP or static routing
  • Site ranges land as temporary routed ranges: usable in Cato policy now, converted to VLANs at firewall retirement
  • Simple bypass of agent connectivity
  • No Cato bandwidth or commercial impact

Cato VLAN termination Phased replacement

  • The socket terminates local VLANs — Cato-managed inter-VLAN routing and visibility from day one
  • Firewall DMZ handoff carries the socket's WAN uplink
  • Internet traffic bypasses the Cato WAN and still breaks out from the firewall during transition
  • Reuses existing local firewall ZIA connectivity
  • No Cato bandwidth or commercial impact
Field evidence

Case study: a UK professional-services firm

Starting point

  • UK-headquartered professional-services firm
  • Zscaler ZIA for internet — GRE tunnels at sites plus ZCC on managed endpoints, with identity
  • Not using ZPA; heavy focus on ZTNA as the goal
  • MPLS WAN protected by legacy firewalls
  • Remote access on a legacy remote-access VPN

Why they moved

  • Extremely unhappy with the Zscaler solution
  • Purchased Zscaler CASB — but it was not working for them
  • Wanted the WAN protected with a ZTNA model, not just internet security
  • Bought Cato as a Zscaler ZIA replacement

The phased cutover

  1. Phase 0 — WAN first, internet untouched

    Sites stayed on ZIA GRE tunnels and endpoints on ZCC. Cato Sockets were deployed and WAN protection built with user awareness before anyone moved off Zscaler: policy-based routing steered WAN traffic to Cato while the default route (internet) still pointed at ZIA.

  2. Phase 1 — SDP to every user

    Cato Client (SDP) rolled out to all users with a split tunnel for RFC 1918 over Cato — not always-on, manually enabled when remote. Internet stayed on ZIA at sites and on ZCC when remote: ZCC and SDP co-existing on the same workstations, dual tunnels while remote.

  3. Phase 2 — Default route to Cato for WAN

    WAN rules completed on the ZTNA model, enforced purely on Cato identities. PBR was removed and the default route migrated to Cato for WAN access; non-user systems (servers, IoT) moved to Cato SWG via the socket on a minimal rule set, keeping visibility and posture. ZIA still protected internet via ZCC — its tunnel now riding inside the Cato socket tunnel on-site.

  4. Phase 3 — Copy SWG policies, prove on a test cohort

    Zscaler SWG policies were copied into Cato SWG. A subset of users had ZCC removed in an on-prem sandbox to confirm functionality — deliberately isolating SDP policy dependencies from the test methodology.

  5. Phase 4 — Always-on SDP, big-bang split-tunnel removal

    SDP switched to always-on (still split-tunnelling RFC 1918, still leaning on ZCC for ZIA), then the split tunnel was removed in one move: SDP became the default route to the internet via the Cato PoP, with ZCC forming its ZIA tunnel through Cato to the Zscaler PoP. ZCC was then removed at convenience — internet traffic flipping from ZIA to Cato SWG per endpoint.

Lessons learned — keep these in the room

This migration worked, but it was not friction-free. SEs who tell this part of the story honestly build more trust than any polished slide.

Unidentified users broke SWG logic

All SDP users were being identified as the “All Unidentified Users” group while user-group logic worked correctly behind the socket — causing SWG policy logic failures. A flaw in Cato's logic (since being fixed), quickly worked around with an exception for the All SDP Users group.

TLS inspection vs Outlook

TLSi caused issues with Outlook, and the customer's change management delayed diagnostic testing — a little over a month of rollout delay. Root cause sat on the customer side and was resolved via a Cato support ticket.

Change windows set the pace

Tight change control and narrow windows made progress extremely slow. Agree the change-window cadence for every phase before the project starts — the technology was never the bottleneck.

Looking forward: integrating the recently released agent-based SDP identity with WMI-based identity to improve the user-awareness experience, and using user/group-based split-tunnel policies (as they release) to simplify Phase-3 test cohorts and make the Phase-4 cutover gradual instead of big-bang.

PoV runbook

How to prove it in the customer's tenant

A Zscaler displacement is too big to PoV whole — so don't try. The pilot that actually de-risks the decision is a bounded co-existence exercise: one cohort of users swapped from ZCC to the Cato Client, the converted internet policy proven at parity on their real traffic, ZIA left authoritative for everyone else — and a clean way back if anything surprises. It is phases 2–3 of the animated journey in miniature, run under the discipline of the PoV framework: agree the success criteria below with the customer before anything is configured, and book the decision meeting the same day.

Prerequisites — agree before anything is configured
  • Success criteria signed off and the decision meeting booked, per the PoV framework.
  • Trial licences confirmed for every capability under test — CASB and TLS inspection if verdict parity is in scope.
  • IdP integration connected, with a dedicated pilot group provisioned over SCIM and visibly syncing before any rule references it.
  • TLS inspection staged first where content verdicts matter — certificate out, bypasses in, then inspection (see TLS Inspection: Staged Rollout Playbook).
  • Scope is the client-based cohort: 10–25 users across two or three archetypes (e.g. an office cohort behind a socket-connected branch, plus remote workers). ZPA / WAN policy and site cutovers are the migration programme above, not this pilot.
  • ZIA stays authoritative for everyone outside the cohort — nothing in this runbook touches the wider estate's forwarding.

Configuration walkthrough

  1. Pick the cohort and capture the baseline

    Monitor → Experience Monitoring Access → Users

    The cohort starts in the dual-agent state this page describes — Cato Client for WAN, ZCC still handling internet. Provision the pilot IdP group, confirm SCIM has synced it, and run a baseline week: Experience Monitoring scores for the cohort (Remote Users tab — score, apps, PoP, TTFB per user) alongside an export of the same users' ZIA logs. That export is the parity reference everything is judged against.

  2. Translate the pilot-scope internet policy — monitor-first

    Security → Internet Firewall

    Recreate only the ZIA URL-filtering and firewall rules the cohort actually hits, scoped to the pilot group as source: Block stays Block, Caution becomes Prompt, and Track = Event on every rule — events are the evidence. Cato's Internet firewall ends in an implicit any-any allow, the same default posture as ZIA, so unmatched traffic behaves identically. Arm blocks only for the agreed test list; everything else runs in monitor until its events prove it. Full construct-by-construct mapping: Zscaler Policy Migration.

  3. Stage TLS inspection for the cohort only

    Security → TLS Inspection

    Deploy the Cato root certificate to cohort devices through GPO/MDM, then add an Inspect rule scoped to the pilot group. Rebuild bypasses deliberately — certificate-pinned applications must be bypass rules, and Cato bypasses Android, Linux and unidentified operating systems automatically — rather than porting the ZIA exemption list wholesale. Without this step, CASB activity and content verdicts cannot reach parity.

  4. Recreate the CASB parity rules

    Security → CASB

    Two rules cover the criterion: a tenant restriction pinning corporate SaaS to the corporate tenant, and a granular block on uploads to personal instances — both in monitor first, promoted for the cohort once the events line up with what ZIA's Cloud App Control was deciding for the same users.

  5. Keep ZIA reachable where the transition needs it

    Network → Sites Network → IP Allocation

    If the estate is running this page's first interim architecture — internet bridged from the Cato PoP to ZIA — configure ZIA as an IPsec IKEv2 site: Cato-allocated IPs for the primary and secondary tunnels, a PSK of up to 64 characters, and up to six active tunnels per HA role for the load-balanced pattern described above (AES-GCM ciphers for tunnels above 100 Mbps). Where sites instead keep their existing firewall's GRE/IPsec into ZIA, there is nothing to build here — the pilot rides the architecture the migration already chose.

  6. Swap the cohort's client — ZCC off, Cato full tunnel on

    Access → Split Tunnel Policy

    Mirror the case study's Phase 3: the cohort swaps, it does not run two SWGs at once. Remove ZCC from pilot devices through the same GPO/MDM channel that deployed it — clearing the ZIA PAC / forwarding profile in the same change — then flip the cohort's internet to Cato with a user-group-scoped split-tunnel rule, so the pilot group's default route goes via the PoP while the rest of the estate keeps its ZCC split-tunnel arrangement untouched.

  7. Run the parity week and build the evidence pack

    Monitor → Events

    Walk the agreed test list with a cohort user: blocked categories, prompted categories, personal versus corporate tenant upload. Filter Events to the converted rules, confirm each verdict is attributed to the named user and rule, and export the filtered view as a dated CSV per criterion (a single export carries up to 250,000 events). Pair each Cato event with its matching ZIA log line; the cohort's pilot-week Experience Monitoring scores against the baseline close the pack.

What good output looks like

Troubleshooting the pilot

🥊

Two agents on one device

Co-existence here means ZCC riding inside the Cato tunnel with ZIA doing the web inspection — never two SWGs inspecting the same flow. If a swapped device still shows ZCC connected, the swap didn't complete: finish the removal before reading any parity events, exactly as the case study's sandboxed cohort test did.

🧾

PAC remnants

After ZCC removal, a browser or GPO still pushing the ZIA PAC file sends traffic to a dead proxy — or silently around Cato. Check proxy settings on the misbehaving device; remove PAC and proxy-enforcement GPOs in the same change as the agent swap, not afterwards.

📌

SSL-pinning breakage

Apps that ZIA quietly exempted start failing the moment TLSi inspects them. Certificate-pinned applications must be TLS bypass rules on Cato. Rebuild the bypass list deliberately from observed TLSi events — the old ZIA exemption list is a clue, not a config.

🏢

SaaS egress-IP allowlists

Tenants conditional-access-pinned to Zscaler egress ranges lock the cohort out the moment egress moves to the Cato PoP. Sweep M365 and other SaaS tenant allowlists before the swap, and add Cato allocated IPs (bound to egress via network-rule NAT) where a fixed source IP is required.

🪪

Cohort rules never match

SCIM sync lag — or SDP users landing in an unidentified-users bucket, the case study's hardest-won lesson — means group-scoped rules silently never fire. Verify membership in Access → Users and prove one event attributes to a named pilot user before the parity week starts.

🌉

Interim tunnel down

IKE parameter or PSK mismatch, NAT-T disabled on the far side, or misaligned traffic selectors are the usual culprits on the ZIA bridge. Work the KB flow: Connection Status for the parameter snapshot, the timeline connection log for history, then reset the tunnel.

Gotchas & the exit plan

Honest caveats — say these before the customer finds them
  • Parity means intent, not identical counts. The two category taxonomies do not line up one-to-one — judge the agreed test list verdict by verdict, never by comparing event volumes.
  • Licence boundaries. CASB verdict parity needs the CASB module and TLS inspection live; DLP parity is a workstream of its own — keep it out of the pilot unless licensed and explicitly scoped.
  • Evidence windows. An Events query spans at most 31 days — export weekly as the framework prescribes, rather than reconstructing at wrap-up.
  • Baseline honesty. The baseline is captured in the dual-agent state — compare like-for-like weeks (same users, same working pattern), or the experience comparison proves nothing.
  • ZPA is out of scope. WAN and private-access policy never changes during this pilot, so there is nothing on that side to unwind.

If the pilot passes, nothing is thrown away: the pilot rules are the first production cohort's policy, the pilot group simply widens, and the programme proceeds up this page's phased cutover. If it must unwind, revert the cohort in reverse order: redeploy ZCC and its forwarding profile/PAC over GPO/MDM (internet verdicts return to ZIA immediately), restore the cohort's previous split-tunnel arrangement so the Cato Client goes back to WAN-only duty, and set the pilot's Internet firewall and CASB rules back to monitor — disable rather than delete, because the conversion work remains an asset for the real migration. The TLSi root certificate is inert and can stay or go with the same tooling. Tear down the interim IPsec site last, and only if nothing else routes through it — it usually belongs to the wider transition, not the pilot. The cohort lands exactly where it started: dual-agent, ZIA-protected, decision de-risked.
