Cloud-Delivered Inbound Protection

Every service you publish to the internet — a partner portal, a B2B API, a legacy web app — is an open door that has to be defended and audited. This use case shows how to publish services without exposing them: an allocated IP at a Cato PoP of your choice, Remote Port Forwarding to the on-prem resource, and inbound IPS scrubbing every request before it ever reaches your equipment — with virtual patching closing new CVEs in days, not maintenance windows.

Business objective

Why this matters

Most organisations still have services that must face the internet from on-premise infrastructure — and every one of them is a target for automated scanning and exploitation from the moment it goes live. The traditional answer is a stack of appliances at the edge: firewalls, WAFs, reverse proxies — each with its own patch cycle, its own downtime window, and its own line in the audit report.

Increasingly the driver is not just security, it is compliance. The frameworks customers are measured against all demand demonstrable controls over public-facing services:

ISO 27001

A standard to establish, implement and maintain information security — including controls over externally exposed systems and their patching.

NIS2

The EU framework for protecting critical infrastructure — ensuring security and resilience, and preventing, deterring and mitigating major incidents.

SOC 2

Auditing of controls on security, availability, confidentiality and privacy — with evidence the auditor can actually inspect.

The Cato solution

Publish the service, not the server

Remote Port Forwarding gives you an allocated IP at a Cato PoP of your choosing — that IP, not your site, is what the world sees. Inbound connections terminate at the PoP, where the SPACE engine and inbound IPS inspect and scrub every request. Flexible access controls filter inbound sources, and only clean traffic is forwarded down the socket’s existing DTLS tunnel to the on-prem resource. No inbound firewall holes, no DMZ, no infrastructure or network topology changes — and no appliances to deploy on site.

Virtual patching

Rapid CVE mitigation — protected before you patch

When a critical CVE lands on a service you publish, the race is between the exploit kits and your next maintenance window. Cato performs virtual patching in the IPS layer of SPACE: Cato Security Research writes the signature and deploys it to every PoP worldwide, without customer involvement and without touching the vulnerable service. The service stays up; the exploit path is closed.

Demo runbook

How to run the demo

Before you demo — dig into the “why”

Ask what they publish today and how it is protected — a DMZ and a WAF appliance? A cloud load balancer? Nothing but a port forward on the firewall? Anchor the demo to the service they name and the framework they are audited against.

  1. Configure an allocated IP at a PoP of your choosing

    Allocate an IP at the PoP nearest the audience — for a UK audience, the London PoP. This static IP becomes the public address of the published service; nothing else about the site is reachable from the internet.

  2. Create the Remote Port Forwarding rule

    Forward HTTP/HTTPS from the allocated IP to a lab web resource — an Azure or AWS tenancy attached to your lab works well (e.g. a web server behind an AWS vSocket site (eu-west-2) or the Azure vSocket hub). Show the inbound source filtering options while you are there: restrict by source IP or country if the service is only for known partners.

  3. Verify inbound IPS is turned on

    Security → IPS

    Confirm IPS is enabled and protecting inbound traffic. Make the point that the signatures are maintained and deployed by Cato Security Research — there is nothing here for the customer to patch or update.

  4. Prove the legitimate path

    From an outside connection (phone hotspot works), browse to the allocated IP and show the published service loading normally — the green lane on the diagram.

  5. Show live protection

    Monitor → Events

    Review IPS events against the allocated IP — internet-facing IPs collect real scanner traffic quickly. Or trigger the IPS deliberately with a known CVE that Cato has a signature for, and watch the block appear as a fully attributed event.

  6. Close on virtual patching

    Open the Rapid CVE Mitigation page and land the message: “When the next critical CVE hits this service, Cato closes the exploit path at every PoP in 0–2 days — no downtime, no change on your side.”

PoV runbook

How to prove it in the customer's tenant

The demo publishes a lab resource from our estate; the PoV publishes one of theirs — a non-production test service behind the pilot socket, fronted by an allocated IP at a PoP of their choosing. The pattern is the deliverable: prove it end to end on the test service, and the production cutover becomes a routine DNS change rather than a leap of faith. Never pilot on the production ingress. Prerequisites — agree before week one
  • A non-production test service behind the pilot socket — a copy of the real app, or any web service the customer owns. Prefer plain HTTP for the pilot: TLS inspection is not performed on inbound traffic, so a cleartext test service lets the IPS see full payloads rather than only what reputation and protocol checks catch.
  • Allocated-IP licence headroom: the number of IPs an account can allocate is set by licence — confirm at least one is free for the pilot before the workshop.
  • PoP choice: pick the PoP nearest the service's audience. Note RPF is not supported on PoPs located in China — plan around it if that geography matters.
  • IPS active for the account, with the inbound direction in scope for the trial.
  • A test DNS name the customer controls (e.g. pilot.<their-domain>) — the production record is never touched during the PoV.
  • Ground rule on testing: any active scanning is performed by the customer's authorised security team, against their own test service, through their own change process. The SE never runs attack tooling against anyone's infrastructure — the KB-documented safe tests and real background scanner traffic carry the evidence.

Configuration walkthrough

  1. Allocate the IP at the agreed PoP

    Network → IP Allocation

    Allocate a static public IP at the chosen PoP (London, for a UK-facing service) — per the KB, allocated IPs are account-specific, not shared between customers, and the number available is determined by licence. Screenshot the allocation, dated — evidence item zero: this address, not the customer's site, is what the internet will see.

  2. Enable Remote Port Forwarding and build the rule

    Security → Remote Port Forwarding

    Enable the service, then create the rule per the KB: the allocated IP and external port on one side, the test service's internal IP and port on the other. Keep the port range to exactly what the service needs — one published port is one criterion; a range is scope creep.

  3. Restrict who can even connect

    Security → Remote Port Forwarding

    In the rule's Remote IPs settings choose one of the two mutually exclusive modes: an Allow List (block everything except listed sources) or a Block List (allow everything except listed sources) — sources can be IP ranges, subnets or countries. For a partner-facing pilot, allow-list the partner ranges plus the customer's own test sources; the dropped-probe criterion falls straight out of this configuration.

  4. Set the inbound IPS posture

    Security → IPS

    On the Protection Policy tab, IPS covers inbound, outbound and WAN directions with Block or Monitor per direction. For a test service the customer can go straight to Block on inbound — nothing production depends on it — but running week one in Monitor and flipping after reviewing events together is the gentler pattern, and the before/after event stream is itself an artefact. Remind them: signatures are written and deployed by Cato Security Research — there is nothing on their side to patch.

  5. Add geo-restriction, if it is a criterion

    Security → IPS

    On the Geo Restriction tab, add an inbound rule for the agreed excluded countries with Event tracking — the KB states inbound geo rules apply to RPF resources. Scope note: geo rules are not applied to traffic from Cato SDP Clients, so this control is about internet sources reaching the published service — never test it from a device running the Client.

  6. Point the test DNS name at the allocated IP

    Have the customer create pilot.<their-domain> as an A record to the allocated IP, with a short TTL. This is deliberately a new record: the production name keeps resolving to today's ingress for the whole PoV, and the eventual cutover is a separate, planned change.

  7. Run the three tests

    Monitor → Events

    From an outside connection on an allow-listed source, browse to the test name and confirm the service loads — then find the allowed events. From a source that is not on the list, try again and show the connection fail with nothing in the backend log. Then let the allocated IP do what internet-facing IPs do: collect real scanner and recon traffic, and review the IPS verdicts against it with the customer. If they want active proof beyond that, their authorised security team can run their standard vulnerability scan against the test service through the published IP, per their own change process — their scanner, their service, their change record.

What good output looks like

The money shot is a single Events filter — the allocated IP — returning two interleaved stories: allowed flows carrying real users to the test service, and blocked rows where scanner traffic and out-of-policy sources died at the PoP. Pair that with the backend's own access log for the same window, which contains only the allowed requests, and the "scrubbed before it arrives" claim from the diagram above becomes a dated, filterable artefact. The page's flow diagram and the Rapid CVE table are the story; this filter is the proof.

Troubleshooting

🌐

Test name resolves to the wrong place

DNS caching — check the record's TTL and query the authoritative server directly to confirm the A record. Keep the pilot on its own test name so stale caches never touch production, and set a short TTL now: it is also the rehearsal for the eventual cutover.

🔌

Service unreachable via the allocated IP

Work outside-in: is the RPF service toggle enabled (it is off by default), does the rule's external IP/port match what you are hitting, is the tester's source on the Allow List, and can the socket actually reach the internal IP and port? Test the internal leg from inside the site before blaming the edge.

🪪

Backend logs show unexpected source addresses

Do not assume the original client IP survives to the backend — verify what the backend actually logs during week one, and enforce source restrictions in the RPF Allow List at the PoP rather than in backend ACLs. If the app needs the true client address for its own logic, that is a finding to record, not to discover at cutover.

🩺

Health checks and monitors go red

External uptime monitors are inbound sources like any other: in Allow List mode their probe ranges are dropped unless listed. Add the monitoring provider's source ranges to the list — and treat their first successful probe as free evidence for the legitimate-path criterion.

🔐

Expecting content verdicts on inbound HTTPS

TLS inspection is not performed on inbound traffic — the KB is explicit. For encrypted flows the IPS works from reputation and protocol-level checks, not decrypted payloads. That is why the pilot runs on a cleartext test service; write the HTTPS scope honestly into the criteria rather than around it.

⏱️

A new rule seems ignored

Cato's testing KB notes an IPS rule can take up to 10 minutes to become effective — wait before diagnosing. And if the allocated IP is also used by IPsec sites, keep UDP 500 and 4500 out of the forwarded port ranges, or the tunnels and the rule will fight.

Gotchas & clean exit

Honest caveats
  • The PoV proves the pattern, not the migration: production cutover is a separate, planned change — TTL run-down on the real record, a rollback path, and its own window. Resist the "it works, just flip prod" moment in week two; write the cutover into the next-steps register instead.
  • Inbound inspection has a stated boundary: no TLS inspection inbound means encrypted payloads are not content-inspected. The criteria table above is written for what the platform states it does — keep it that way in front of the customer.
  • Licence and geography edges: allocated-IP count is licence-bound, and RPF is not supported on China PoPs.
  • No attack tooling, ever: the SE demonstrates with legitimate requests, allow-list behaviour and the background scanner traffic every public IP attracts. Anything more active belongs to the customer's authorised security team, against their own test service, under their own change process.

Exit: at wrap-up, remove the test DNS record, delete the RPF rule (or leave it as the seed of the production design if the PoV converts — that decision is the point), release or repurpose the allocated IP against the licence, and return any geo or IPS changes to their agreed end-state. Administration → Audit Trail holds the record of everything the PoV added and removed — and for the auditor conversation this page started with, that trail is itself the closing exhibit.
