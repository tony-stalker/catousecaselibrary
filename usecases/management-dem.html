Proactive Digital Experience Monitoring

“The app is slow” tickets die in the gap between the network team, the security team and the app team. Cato Digital Experience Monitoring shows the experience each user actually gets, per app and hop by hop — device, Wi-Fi, last mile, PoP, backbone, application — so IT sees the failing segment in seconds instead of convening a war room.

Business objective

Why this matters

Users don’t experience your network, your security stack or your SaaS contracts — they experience a Teams call that stutters, a CRM page that hangs, a file that won’t upload. When that happens, the ticket says “the app is slow”, and the truth is buried somewhere along a path that crosses half a dozen ownership boundaries: the laptop, the home Wi-Fi, the ISP, the middle mile, the provider’s cloud, the application itself.

The objective: answer “where is it slow, and for whom?” from one console, in seconds — and increasingly, spot the degradation and act on it before the first user picks up the phone.

The problem

Where “the app is slow” tickets go to die

Every segment of the user-to-app path has a different owner, a different tool and a different definition of “fine”. Traditional monitoring watches infrastructure the organisation owns — which is precisely the part of the path a hybrid worker’s traffic barely touches.

Path segmentWho owns itHow it’s diagnosed today
Device (CPU, memory, Wi-Fi signal)Desktop / endpoint teamRemote-control session, ask the user to reboot
Home or branch Wi-Fi / LANUser at home; network team in the officeGuesswork — “try moving closer to the router”
ISP last mileThe ISP — “no fault found”Speed-test screenshots, circular support calls
Middle mile / internet coreNobody you can callTraceroute fragments, blamed by default
The applicationSaaS vendorStatus page says green, case closed

The war-room pattern

Network, security and app teams each check their own dashboard, each declare their layer healthy, and the ticket bounces until the user gives up or the symptom disappears. Mean time to innocence is fast; mean time to resolution is not.

The reactive pattern

IT learns about degradation from the helpdesk queue. By the time tickets cluster, a site has been limping on a lossy circuit for days — with no baseline to prove when it started or evidence to hold the ISP to.

Discovery question

“When a user says Teams is slow, how many tools — and how many teams — does it take you to say with confidence whether it’s the laptop, the Wi-Fi, the ISP or the app?”

The Cato solution

The whole path, one view, per user

Cato is in a unique position to answer the question: the Cato Client on the device, the Socket at the site, the PoP and the global private backbone are all one platform — so Cato observes every hop the traffic actually takes. Digital Experience Monitoring turns that into an experience score per user and per application, decomposed segment by segment: device health → Wi-Fi/LAN → last mile → PoP → backbone → application. The failing segment is measured, not inferred.

User → app traffic, measured at every hop
One degraded segment, six healthy ones: the failing segment is visible, not inferred. Here the fault is the ISP last mile — not the laptop, not Cato, not the app.

What this changes for IT operations

🎯

Fault domain in one view

Device, Wi-Fi, last mile, PoP, backbone and app health side by side — the conversation starts at the failing segment, not at “whose fault is it?”.

📉

Fewer helpdesk escalations

First-line support reads the experience score and segment breakdown directly, so tickets stop bouncing between network, security and app teams.

📄

Evidence for ISP disputes

Per-segment last-mile metrics — loss, latency, jitter over time — turn “no fault found” into a documented circuit case the ISP has to answer.

📈

Baseline and trend, per site and user

Experience is tracked continuously, so you see degradation forming — and can act — before users call it in.

Demo runbook

How to run the demo

Before you demo — dig into the “why”

Ask how a “Teams is slow” ticket flows through their organisation today: who touches it, how long it stays open, how often it closes as “no fault found”. Anchor every step of the demo back to that answer.

  1. Open Experience Monitoring and rank by experience

    Monitor → Experience Monitoring

    Sort users by experience score, worst first. Point out that this is the helpdesk queue before it happens — the users at the top are the ones about to call.

  2. Drill into a poor Teams call

    Pick a low-scoring user — say Marcus, connected via the Mobile Office LTE site — and open a degraded Microsoft Teams session. The ticket would have said “Teams is broken”; the console shows exactly what his call experienced.

  3. Isolate the failing segment

    Walk the per-segment breakdown for the session: device health fine, LAN fine — the loss and jitter sit on the last-mile segment between the site and the PoP. One screen, no traceroutes, no war room.

  4. Contrast a healthy user

    Open a well-scoring user — say Priya, connecting through the London PoP — on the same application. Same app, same Cato policy, green on every segment: the difference is the access circuit, and the data proves it.

  5. Show the trend, not just the moment

    Switch to the site-level view for Mobile Office and show experience over time — when the degradation started, whether it correlates with hours or load, and what the baseline looked like before.

  6. Set the follow-up

    Close the loop: export the per-segment evidence and raise the ISP ticket with loss and latency history attached. “No fault found” doesn’t survive contact with this data — and no user had to report anything for IT to get here.
