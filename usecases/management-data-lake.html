Event Logs & the Cato Data Lake

Every tracked policy hit, connection and detection across the platform lands as an event in the Cato Data Lake — and the licence that sizes it is one of the few Cato conversations with real arithmetic in it. Size the units too low and the busiest hour's excess events may be discarded; export everything unfiltered and the SIEM bill does the complaining instead. This page covers how events are generated, what a Data Lake unit actually buys, how to estimate units from real history or the KB's tables, and how to get the feed out — which Cato does free of charge.

Business objective

Why this matters

Three conversations depend on understanding this pipeline. Sizing at order time: Data Lake units are licensed on peak events per hour, so an estimate has to exist before the first renewal conversation. Avoiding silent loss: events beyond the licensed hourly rate may be discarded for the remainder of the hour — undersizing is not a performance problem, it is missing evidence. Budgeting the SIEM: exporting the full firehose to external storage is free on Cato's side, but downstream SIEMs typically bill by ingested volume — trimming the feed is a policy exercise, not a procurement one.

The pipeline

From policy hit to Data Lake unit

Events come from the services — Networking, Security, Access and the rest — and the volume is a function of policy. Cato retains a core set of key security and connectivity events for every customer; beyond that, each policy rule's tracking option decides whether a hit becomes an event. The licence then defines the hourly rate limit: units of 2.5 million events per hour, licensed on the peak — a quiet hour does not bank capacity for a busy one, and events in excess of the licensed rate may be discarded for the remainder of the hour.

Retention is the second dimension of the same unit: variants of 3, 6 or 12 months, and the chosen variant applies to all units — mixing retention periods is not possible. After the retention period, event data is deleted from the Cato Cloud and cannot be recovered. One caveat worth quoting exactly: if a customer pays for additional retention, no allowance is made for the free retention — all event retention becomes chargeable. The model applies to contracts and renewals from 1 January 2024, and some contracts include terms that differ.

🕐

Peak, not average

Units define the maximum events per hour. A period of fewer events has no bearing on future hours — size for the busiest hour of the busiest day, then verify against real history.

🗄️

Retention is a variant, not a mix

Three-, six- and twelve-month unit variants exist, and the chosen variant applies to every unit on the account. There is no blending of retention periods across units.

🧮

📤

The KB's worked example — and an honest footnote

The KB works one example against these tables: 3 Gbps of total bandwidth reads off as a 5-million-event peak, CASB and RBI add one unit, and the customer buys "three more Data Lake Storage units of the appropriate duration". But the example's SDP line reads 5,000 clients as "an additional estimated peak of two and a half million events per hour" — while the SDP table above puts 5,000 clients in the 3K–7K band at 5,000,000. The tables are the normative instrument ("Follow this procedure to calculate requirements from the tables") — but for the 3K–7K band the estimator below follows the KB's own worked example at 2.5M, not the table's 5M: it is the KB's own pricing of 5,000 clients, it keeps a mid-size user estate inside the included unit, and it makes the estimator reproduce the example's arithmetic exactly (5 + 2.5 = 7.5M, three units with CASB and RBI). The table's 5M stands as the conservative reading if a customer prefers it.

Storage and export volume — the rough conversion

Data volume is not used in the calculation or purchase of units and is not reported by the CMA — units are bought on events per hour alone. But customers planning an export to external storage or a SIEM can use the KB's rough conversion: one Data Lake unit (2.5 million events per hour) is very roughly equivalent to 180 GB per month of data storage. The KB's table, verbatim:

Derived from the KB's own figures — not a published number — 2.5M events/hour for a month at 180 GB works out to roughly 100 bytes per event on average; Cato publishes no per-event-type sizes. The KB flags the whole conversion as "a very rough estimate": an account sized for occasional peaks stores far less than one generating events at a consistently high rate.

Unit estimator

Estimate units and export volume

This estimator implements the KB's five-step procedure over the published tables — nothing more. It follows the tables where the KB's own worked example reads lower, computes storage linearly where the KB's table row three does not, and refuses to extrapolate beyond the published bands. The procedure it automates:

  1. Read the estimated peak events per hour from the Total Bandwidth table.
  2. Read the estimated peak events per hour from the SDP Clients table.
  3. Add the two.
  4. Divide by 2.5 million and round up — the units for bandwidth and SDP Clients.
  5. Using multiple high-event services such as CASB or LAN Firewall? Add 1 Data Lake unit.

Why the estimator reads a floor-to-ceiling range: a real estate at roughly 42 Gbps with ~43K SDP users, running WAN Firewall, SWG, ATP and CASB, peaks at about 31M events per hour (field observation, Sep 2026, anonymised) — far below the 55M+ the additive tables would suggest, because site bandwidth and SDP users are largely the same traffic. The floor takes the larger of the two bands; the ceiling is the KB's additive sum. Size the presales conversation from the floor, then verify against the account's own Events chart.

Export & ingestion

Getting events out — free of charge

Cato supports consuming events in the CMA itself, as a high-scale feed to cloud storage (AWS S3 and Azure Blob Storage; the push cadence below is published for S3), and by pull through the eventsFeed API — and forwarding data to external cloud storage and SIEMs carries no additional Cato cost. The choice is about mechanics:

AWS S3 — the push feed

Continuous push — unlike the eventsFeed API, which requires pulling data from Cato and can be affected by rate limiting. Uploads land every 60 seconds, or whenever more than 9.5 MB of uncompressed data accumulates, as .GZ files over HTTPS. Per-integration filters select events by type or sub-type (multiple filters AND together; the default is everything). Only S3 regions with the Security Token Service active are supported — the China region is not — and the IAM role trusts Cato's documented integration ARN with the CMA account ID as the ExternalId.

Azure Blob & the eventsFeed API

Azure Blob Storage is the equivalent feed target on the other cloud; the KB confirms it as a high-scale feed destination. The eventsFeed API is the pull path — the right tool for SIEM connectors and custom pipelines, with one budget lever worth knowing: enabling gzip compression on API requests reduces the required storage by up to 95%. Up to three Event Integrations can be defined per account.

Trimming the feed before the SIEM bills it

The KB's ingestion workflow cuts volume at the source, in three passes of the Events page's field analysis:

  1. Eliminate by Event Type

    In most cases, the vast majority of generated events are Security events. If Security events are not needed downstream, filtering them out of the integration or eventsFeed query is the single biggest cut available — the KB notes that eliminating other event types is not likely to have a significant impact on the total.

  2. Eliminate by Sub-Type

    Within Security events, Internet and WAN Firewall events represent the vast majority for many accounts. Filter sub-types the SOC does not consume.

  3. Eliminate by Application — the Allow-no-Event technique

    A significant share of firewall events often comes from applications nobody needs logged — DNS is the classic case. Create firewall rules with the Allow action and no Event for that traffic, so the events are never generated. The KB recommends a Custom Application defined with the destination IPs of DNS servers that need no tracking, allowed by a single rule. Other candidates it names: ICMP, SNMP, Windows Update, Microsoft Teams and Zoom.

Tuning in practice — real-world low-value traffic

Two different levers, two different bills. A rule set to Allow with no Event stops the event being generated — that is the lever that reduces the peak rate and therefore Data Lake units. A filter on an Event Integration or eventsFeed query stops the event being ingested — that trims the SIEM bill, but the event still counts against the hourly rate limit. Presales conversations routinely conflate the two; keep them separate and the sizing gets smaller honestly.

Two practices worth adopting on every account
  • Enable the integration even without a target: switching on integration with Cato events under Resources → Event Integrations lets Cato analyse the account's feed when troubleshooting with support — without it, that data is not available.
  • XDR stories are silent by default: no story events are generated until an XDR Response Policy rule is configured — build the rule before promising story events in the SIEM.
Demo runbook

How to run the demo

The demo in one sentence

Read the account's real event peaks off the chart, walk the Popular Fields elimination that shrinks them, then show where the feed leaves the platform — turning a licensing abstraction into fifteen minutes of concrete arithmetic.

  1. Open on the Events chart and find the peaks

    Home → Events

    Set a long window and look at the events-over-time chart. The question the licence asks is visible here: what is the tallest bar? An account peaking at 400K events per hour sits comfortably inside the included unit; one exceeding 2.5M in every hour needs an additional unit before events start being discarded.

  2. Hover for exact heights — and mind the granularity

    Home → Events

    Hover a bar to read its exact height. Then change the time window and point out the Time Series Granularity: the period each bar represents changes with the window, so a "peak hour" read off a 90-day chart is not the same measurement as one off a 24-hour chart. The KB's advice: prefer a long analysis window, and verify the granularity before quoting a number.

  3. Walk the Popular Fields elimination

    Home → Events

    Use the Popular Fields panel to show the event-type distribution — Security events dominating is the normal picture. Drill into Sub-Type (Internet and WAN Firewall carrying most of it) and then Application, where DNS usually owns a surprising share. This is the trimming workflow from the export section, demonstrated live on real data.

  4. Show where the feed leaves the platform

    Resources → Event Integrations

    Open the Event Integrations page and make the best-practice point: enable integration with Cato events even if no external target exists yet — it lets Cato analyse the feed when troubleshooting with support. Note the account-level cap of three integrations.

  5. Open a configured integration and its filter

    Resources → Event Integrations

    Show an integration's configuration: the target, and the event filter selecting types and sub-types (multiple filters AND together; no filter means everything is uploaded). This is where the Popular Fields analysis becomes configuration — the SIEM receives the trimmed feed, not the firehose.

  6. Close on the commercial frame

    Three sentences to land: every account includes one Data Lake unit — 2.5M events per hour, three months of retention — but it only stands alone: the moment an account licenses units, all units are chargeable, including the first. Additional units raise the peak rate or extend retention to 6 or 12 months, and one variant applies to all units. Export to their own storage or SIEM costs nothing on the Cato side — the sizing conversation is about their peak hour, and the chart from step one already answered it.

Configuration walkthrough

  1. Baseline the event history

    Home → Events

    Set the window to the full pilot period, note the Time Series Granularity, and hover the tallest bars for exact heights. Record the peak events per hour and the date it occurred — this number drives everything else in the runbook.

  2. Turn the measurement into a sizing note

    Divide the measured peak by 2.5 million and round up; add one unit if high-event services such as CASB, RBI or the LAN Firewall are enabled; pick the retention variant the customer's compliance posture needs, remembering it applies to every unit. Quote the floor-to-ceiling range and state that all units are chargeable once licensing begins — the included unit only stands alone. One page, arithmetic shown.

  3. Create the S3 integration

    Resources → Event Integrations

    Enable integration with Cato events, then add the S3 integration: bucket, region, and the IAM role ARN whose trust policy names Cato's integration ARN with the CMA account ID as the ExternalId. Leave the filter empty for the first landing — prove the pipe before trimming it.

  4. Watch the objects land, then validate one

    Within minutes the bucket should show .gz objects arriving every 60 seconds, or sooner when 9.5 MB of uncompressed data accumulates. List the bucket, screenshot the timestamps, download one object and open it — the event rows inside are the end-to-end proof. If a browser decompresses the file but keeps the .GZ extension, rename it to .LOG or .TXT before opening.

  5. Apply the trimming plan

    Resources → Event Integrations

    Set the integration filter to the agreed event types and sub-types (filters AND together). Where the Popular Fields analysis found chatty applications nobody needs logged, create Allow-and-no-Event firewall rules — the Custom Application holding the DNS server IPs is the KB's worked pattern. Compare object volume before and after.

  6. Run the gzip comparison

    Resources → Service API Keys

    Pull the same eventsFeed sample twice — once plain, once with gzip compression enabled on the request — and record both sizes. The KB's claim is a reduction of up to 95%; the customer's own ratio is the number that belongs in their SIEM budget.

Troubleshooting

🪣

No objects in the bucket

Check the IAM role's trust policy names Cato's documented integration ARN and carries the CMA account ID as the ExternalId, and that the bucket's region has the Security Token Service active — the China region is not supported at all.

🔢

Exported counts differ from the Events page

Expected: the Events page total can be rounded, and multiple occurrences of the same event within one minute are combined into a single exported log. Minor discrepancies are by design, not loss.

🕳️

An expected event type is missing

Check the policy rule's tracking option — rules generate events only when tracking is set, and an earlier Allow-no-Event trimming rule may already be suppressing exactly what you are looking for.

📖

No XDR story events in the feed

Stories generate no events by default. Configure an XDR Response Policy rule and re-test — this is a configuration gap, not an export fault.

🚦

The integration will not save

Count the existing Event Integrations — the account cap is three. Consolidate targets or retire an unused integration before adding the PoV's.

📉

The measured peak exceeds the licensed rate

Events beyond the licensed hourly rate may be discarded for the remainder of the hour — which also silently caps what the PoV can measure. Raise the sizing conversation before reading further numbers off the chart.

Gotchas & clean exit

Honest caveats
  • Pricing is commercial: unit prices are not published, and the KB notes some contracts include terms that differ from the published model — the sizing note states units, never prices. Confirm commercials with Cato.
  • One variant for all units: retention variants cannot be mixed, and paying for additional retention makes all event retention chargeable — no allowance is made for the free retention.
  • Deleted means deleted: after the retention period, event data is removed from the Cato Cloud and cannot be recovered — the export feed is the only long-term archive.
  • The 180 GB conversion is rough: volume estimates in the evidence doc should carry the KB's own caveat, and the ≈100 bytes-per-event average is derived arithmetic, not a published figure.
  • Azure Blob mechanics: the KB confirms Azure Blob Storage as a feed target but this page does not claim a cadence or size trigger for it — prove that path in the tenant if it is the customer's cloud.

Exit: remove or disable the PoV's Event Integration (freeing one of the three slots), revoke the API key used for the gzip test, and have the customer delete the IAM role and bucket policy on their side. Leave the trimming rules only if the customer wants to keep them — they are ordinary firewall rules, documented in the evidence pack. Export the bucket listings, sizing note and chart screenshots before tenant access ends.
