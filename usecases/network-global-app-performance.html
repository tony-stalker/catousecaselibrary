Global Application & UCaaS Performance

Voice, video and SaaS are unforgiving of latency and loss — and the public internet between continents delivers both, unpredictably, while global MPLS is unaffordable at that reach. This use case shows how traffic enters the nearest Cato PoP and rides the private backbone — accelerated, loss-protected and QoS-prioritised — so a user in Sydney gets the same application experience as one sitting next to the London DC.

Business objective

Why this matters

The applications the business feels most — Teams and Zoom calls, video meetings, M365 and the rest of the SaaS estate — are exactly the ones most sensitive to latency, jitter and packet loss. A file sync that runs 20 % slower is an annoyance; a choppy voice call with the customer is a reputation problem, escalated the same afternoon.

The objective: give every user, in every region, predictable performance for real-time and SaaS applications — without buying a global MPLS network to get it.

The problem

Distance is the enemy — and you don't own the middle

Within a metro, the internet is usually fine. Between continents it is a chain of providers you have no contract with, each making its own routing decisions. The traditional answer — international MPLS — solves predictability but at a price and provisioning timescale that rules it out for global reach, and it never covered SaaS or remote users anyway.

The public internet

  • Best-effort, hot-potato routing across many providers
  • Latency, jitter and loss vary hour to hour — worst on long haul
  • Peering congestion you can't see and can't escalate
  • No SLA — nobody owns your Sydney→London path end to end
  • TCP throughput collapses over high-latency, lossy paths

Global MPLS

  • Predictable — but unaffordable at intercontinental scale
  • Months to provision a new international circuit
  • No on-ramp for SaaS, cloud or remote users
  • QoS stops at the provider edge, per-region contracts
  • Capacity upgrades are slow, disruptive and expensive
Discovery question

“When someone in Sydney says their Teams calls to London are choppy, what do you do today — and when the problem sits in the middle mile, between providers, who do you even call?”

The Cato solution

Nearest PoP in, private backbone across

Every site, user and cloud instance connects to its nearest Cato PoP. From there, traffic rides the global private backbone — PoPs interconnected across multiple Tier-1 carriers, with the optimal route chosen continuously and a 99.999 % uptime SLA between PoPs. Every PoP runs the full software stack, so TCP proxy acceleration, packet-loss mitigation and app- and identity-aware QoS are applied in the path, end to end. SaaS traffic uses smart egress: it exits the backbone at the PoP closest to the application instance, not back at head office.

What the PoPs do to the traffic

TCP proxy acceleration

The PoP terminates the TCP session locally and proxies it across the backbone, so window scaling isn't throttled by end-to-end round-trip time. Long-haul file transfers stop crawling.

Packet-loss mitigation

Loss on the path is recovered between PoPs rather than punished with end-to-end retransmits — the difference between a glitch nobody notices and a robotic voice call.

Smart egress for SaaS

SaaS traffic exits at the PoP closest to the application instance — a Sydney user reaches M365 via the front door nearest the tenant, not via a UK breakout.

What the customer gets

📞

Predictable UCaaS quality

Voice and video are classified and prioritised end to end — at the socket, at the ingress PoP and across the backbone — so calls survive the busy hour.

🚀

Faster long-haul transfers

TCP acceleration turns intercontinental throughput from a function of round-trip time into a function of bandwidth — replication and large file moves finish sooner.

🌍

No per-region tuning

One backbone, one policy. There are no regional appliances to tune, no WAN optimisers to maintain, no “APAC exception” configs to drift.

🪪

Per-app QoS tied to identity

Bandwidth priorities key on the application and the user — the exec's Zoom call and the payroll upload win over a background backup, per person, globally.

Demo runbook

How to run the demo

Before you demo — anchor on their pain

Ask which applications get the complaints and from which regions. If the answer includes Teams, Zoom or “anything from APAC / the Americas”, this demo lands itself — use their app and their far site in every step below.

  1. Show how priorities are defined

    Network → Bandwidth Management

    Walk the priority tiers: voice in P1, video conferencing in P2, bulk and backup traffic below. Point out that priorities key on application and identity, and that the same policy is enforced at the socket and at every PoP — no per-region QoS configs.

  2. Live proof: a call from the far side of the world

    Home → Experience Monitoring

    Make a Teams or Zoom call from a far site — the Sydney-homed aws-sydney-ipsec to London works well — then open the application drill-down in Experience Monitoring: experience scored per user and site, with the latency and TTFB behind the score. (App Analytics shows usage and risk — the experience scoring lives in Experience Monitoring.)

  3. Separate last mile from middle mile

    Monitor → Topology

    Click the Sydney site and show last-mile metrics alongside backbone (PoP-to-PoP) metrics. This is the fault-domain question from discovery, answered on one screen: you can now see whether a bad hour was the local ISP or the long haul.

  4. Show the optimisations as policy

    Network → Network Rules

    Open a WAN rule — file transfer to the London DC is a good example — and show the TCP acceleration and packet-loss mitigation toggles. Optimisation is a per-rule policy decision, not an appliance you deploy and tune per region.

  5. Contrast with the internet path

    Narrate the alternative: Sydney→London over the public internet crosses multiple providers, each hot-potato routing onto the next, with congestion nobody owns. On Cato it is one provider's backbone, entering at the Sydney PoP and exiting at the London PoP, under a 99.999 % SLA — the same argument for every region pair they care about.

  6. Close on SaaS with smart egress

    Finish on M365 or their main SaaS app: traffic exits at the PoP closest to the app instance rather than backhauling to a regional breakout — global SaaS performance without local internet breakout projects in every country.

PoV runbook

How to prove it in the customer's tenant

The demo narrates the backbone; the PoV measures it — a long-haul application-performance pilot between two real geographies in the customer's estate, run inside the frame from Running a Cato PoV: criteria and the measurement methodology agreed at the scope workshop before anything is configured, a baseline week before any comparison run, and every number captured dated against its criterion. This page owns the long-haul story — latency, TTFB, acceleration and egress across the middle mile. Last-mile resilience, failover and QoS under congestion belong to the SD-WAN PoV runbook; run the two together for a full network PoV, but keep their criteria separate on the register. The examples below use London ↔ Sydney — substitute the pair of geographies the complaints actually come from.

Prerequisites — agree before week one
  • Both ends of a real long-haul path: a socket or IPsec site at each geography — a London DC socket and a Sydney IPsec or vSocket site is the classic pair. The far end can live in a cloud region if that is where the users are.
  • A named pilot application and a named SaaS app, chosen at the workshop from where the complaints come — not picked mid-PoV to flatter the numbers.
  • The legacy path stays alive: side-by-side measurement needs today's internet/VPN route available for the whole PoV. Agree who runs the far-end tests and in which change windows.
  • Test harness: a repeatable single-stream transfer (a large file over SMB/HTTPS, or single-stream iperf), a scripted TTFB probe (curl -w against the pilot app), and the Socket WebUI speed test for validating the lines themselves (last-mile measurement guidance).
  • Licences: SD-WAN site licences for both ends. Base Experience Monitoring needs nothing extra; application-specific UCaaS drill-downs need the DEM add-on — check before a criterion depends on them.
  • Transports: keep pilot flows on the Cato Cloud transport — TCP acceleration is not supported on Alternative-WAN or off-cloud transports, so a pilot path pinned to Alt-WAN cannot prove criterion three.
  • Not needed — and actively parked: TLS inspection. Enabling TLSi activates the TCP proxy for all TLS traffic, which would erase the acceleration-off baseline run. Stage it in a security PoV afterwards via the TLS inspection playbook.

Configuration walkthrough

  1. Connect both ends and record the PoP homing

    Monitor → Topology

    Onboard the two pilot sites and confirm each connects to its nearest PoP — note which PoPs, because the segment between them is the part of the path Cato owns and the part the whole PoV measures. Capture the topology view on day one: two sites, two nearby PoPs, the backbone between them. That screenshot is evidence item zero.

  2. Write the measurement plan before touching policy

    A performance PoV lives or dies on fair methodology, so fix it in writing at the workshop and never bend it mid-flight:

    The fairness rules — agree them, print them, follow them
    • Same object, cache-busted: fetch the same file/URL on both paths with a unique query string per run, so neither path is quietly served by a CDN edge.
    • Same hour, both paths: every backbone run is paired with an internet-path run inside the same window — never Tuesday's backbone against Friday's internet.
    • Cold runs: no warm caches, no resumed transfers; new session per run.
    • Repeat across days: at least three days, including the far site's busy hour — a single good run proves nothing about consistency.
    • Single stream for the acceleration test, parallel streams recorded separately — and only ever compared like with like.
    • Raw outputs kept: timestamps, transfer logs and probe output into the evidence register on the day, not summarised from memory later.

  3. Steer the pilot app with a network rule

    Network → Network Rules

    Create a WAN rule matching the pilot application between the two sites, with Active TCP Acceleration left off for the baseline week — the rule makes the PoP act as a TCP proxy for matching traffic, splitting the long connection into client-to-PoP, PoP-to-PoP and PoP-to-destination legs, each with its own short RTT and a window sized to RTT × bandwidth (Cato TCP acceleration and best practices). Network Rules are ordered, first match wins — keep the pilot rule specific and above anything broad, and watch its hit count to prove the flow matches (Configuring Network Rules).

  4. Point the SaaS egress at the app's front door

    Network → Network Rules

    For the named SaaS app, add an internet rule using Route via PoP — it "lets you select the egress PoP location from which the traffic is sent to the Internet" — and choose the PoP nearest the application instance (or NAT, to egress via a specific allocated IP at that PoP, if the customer needs a fixed address for tenant allowlisting). The far-region user now rides the backbone to a front door beside the app instead of crossing the public internet to a regional breakout.

  5. Run the baseline week

    Network → Sites → Site Monitoring → Real Time

    Before enabling any optimisation, let both ends run for a week: the Transport tab graphs in-tunnel Distance (round-trip to the Cato Cloud), jitter, packet loss and throughput per link (real-time site analytics). In the same windows, run the scripted probes over the legacy path. This week is half the evidence — the internet path's hour-to-hour variance against the backbone's flat line is the consistency criterion, and no staged comparison can reconstruct it later.

  6. Run the evidence days

    Home → Experience Monitoring

    Enable Active TCP Acceleration on the pilot rule and run the A/B pairs per the measurement plan: timed single-stream transfers off/on, TTFB probes over both paths in the same hour, and the SaaS egress comparison from the far site. Experience Monitoring scores the pilot app continuously through it all — TTFB, TCP connect, TLS connect and HTTP latency against per-app thresholds (Experience Monitoring) — so the human story ("Sydney stopped complaining") lands with a measured trend behind it. Every capture goes into the register dated, against its criterion, on the day.

What good output looks like

By the wrap-up the register should hold five artefact sets: the same-hour RTT table for both paths across several days; the pilot app's Experience Monitoring trend with the before/after windows marked; the acceleration off/on transfer pairs with their rule hit counts; the Route via PoP rule beside the SaaS app's far-site TTFB improvement; and the baseline-week jitter/loss graphs against the internet-path spot checks. Experience Monitoring is the thread through all of it — per-site and per-app scores, drillable from account level down to the far site, with TTFB as the headline number the customer's own users would recognise.

Troubleshooting

🗄️

The internet run "won"

Almost always cached CDN content — the test object came from an edge node ten milliseconds away on one path and from origin on the other. Cache-bust every fetch with a unique query string and compare origin-to-origin, or accept you measured the CDN, not the path.

🕑

Results swing run to run

Different test hours mean a different internet — hot-potato routing and peering congestion change through the day. Only same-window pairs count; discard any run whose partner on the other path is missing, and re-run rather than average across hours.

🛤️

Acceleration A/B shows nothing

Check the transport first: TCP acceleration is not supported on Alternative-WAN or off-cloud paths, and it does nothing for UDP. Then check the flow really matches the pilot rule (hit count), and that the endpoints aren't already window-tuned to fill the path.

🎛️

The "off" run wasn't off

TLS inspection activates the TCP proxy for all TLS traffic regardless of the rule toggle, and complex rules or egress-IP rules can activate it automatically. Keep TLSi out of the pilot scope and the pilot rule simple, or the baseline leg of the A/B is silently accelerated too.

🧵

Parallel streams hide the story

Enough parallel streams will fill a long-haul pipe with no help at all — the window-scaling win shows single-stream, which is how real file copies and replication behave. Record single-stream and parallel results separately and never compare one against the other.

📉

Speed tests disappoint

The DTLS tunnel adds overhead, so raw synthetic throughput through the tunnel can read below the naked line speed. Validate the line with the Socket WebUI speed test, then judge the PoV on application outcomes — completion times and TTFB — not synthetic Mbps.

Gotchas & exit

Honest caveats to set at the workshop
  • Never promise percentage improvements — measure them. The gain depends on distance, loss, application chattiness and what the internet path happens to be doing that week. Write criteria as "measured side by side, methodology stated", never "40 % faster" — a promised number that under-delivers loses a PoV the evidence was winning.
  • The 99.999 % SLA is PoP-to-PoP. The last mile at each end is still the local ISP — which is the SD-WAN PoV's story to prove, not this one's. Keep the two fault domains separate in the criteria wording.
  • Experience Monitoring has edges: application performance metrics are calculated for outbound TCP traffic only, history caps at three months, and data can lag by up to 30 minutes — capture dated screenshots at each weekly sync, and don't put a UDP-only app behind criterion two.
  • Egress location is a policy choice, not magic: Route via PoP helps when the far-region user is crossing the internet to a distant breakout today. If their breakout is already local to the app instance, this criterion will show parity — check the current path at the workshop before promising a delta.
Unwinding cleanly at the end

Delete the pilot network rules — the steering rule, the acceleration toggle and the Route via PoP/NAT egress rules — so no PoV-era routing decision lingers into production unexamined, and re-run one probe pair to confirm behaviour reverted. Decommission or convert the pilot sites per the commercial agreement. The measurement plan and the evidence register survive: the same-hour tables walk straight into the decision meeting, and the methodology becomes the template for the rollout's acceptance tests.
